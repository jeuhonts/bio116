Membrane proteins and coiled coils: where generic predictors need your help

AlphaFold predicts one chain at a time, in a vacuum. It has no lipid bilayer and doesn't know how many copies of a chain assemble. Membrane proteins and coiled coils are the two classes where that matters most. You'll find transmembrane helices, predict which side of the membrane each loop is on, see why β-barrels escape hydropathy plots, and read the heptad code that decides whether a leucine zipper forms a dimer, trimer or tetramer. Everything runs in your browser, even offline.

Step 1 of 10

Why these proteins are special

In the H-Ras tutorial a single soluble chain folded well and pLDDT told you where to trust it. Two things break that simple picture.

Membrane proteins

Roughly a quarter of human genes encode them, and many drugs act on them. The model has no membrane and no lipids: AlphaFold learned what membrane proteins look like from the PDB, but it never places a bilayer, so you must work out where the membrane is and which side each loop faces. Membrane proteins are also under-represented in the PDB because they are hard to crystallise.

Coiled coils

Two to seven α-helices wind around each other. The sequence pattern is easy to spot, but the register (which residue sits in the core) and the number of helices are hard. AlphaFold only models the copies you give it: submit one chain and you get one helix, submit three and you get a trimer, whether or not it's the real state.

The theme of this page: a predictor answers the question you ask it. For these classes you have to supply part of the answer yourself: where the membrane is, which way the protein points, and how many chains there are.

Step 2 of 10

Find the transmembrane helices

The hydrocarbon core of a bilayer is about 30 Å thick. An α-helix rises 1.5 Å per residue, so about 20 hydrophobic residues span it. Averaging Kyte–Doolittle hydropathy over a 19-residue window and calling windows above 1.6 finds many transmembrane (TM) helices. The helices are called one at a time: take the highest window, mark its 19 residues, then take the next-highest window that doesn't overlap.

Show the answer

The missed bacteriorhodopsin helices are at 95–114 and 191–210 (precursor numbering). Helix C (95–114) carries Asp98 and Asp109, which are Asp85 and Asp96 of the mature protein, the proton-pumping residues. Charged or polar residues that do a job inside the membrane pull the window average below 1.6. In glycophorin A the extra "helix" at 5–23 is the signal peptide, a hydrophobic stretch that targets the protein to the membrane and is then cut off. Hydropathy can't tell the two apart. Phobius and DeepTMHMM model signal peptides explicitly.

Step 3 of 10

Which way does it point? The positive-inside rule

Knowing where the helices are isn't enough; you also need the topology, meaning which loops are inside the cell and which are outside. Von Heijne noticed that the short loops on the cytoplasmic side are rich in lysine and arginine (the "positive-inside" rule). Loops alternate sides, so you add up K + R near the helix ends on side A (the N-terminal side and every second loop after it) and on side B. The side with more positive charge is predicted to be inside.

Try this. For each protein, first predict with your own helices, then press Use reference helices. When does the rule fail because the helices were wrong, and when does it fail on its own?

Show the answer

With reference helices the rule gets bacteriorhodopsin (N-out), rhodopsin (N-out), LacY (N-in) and glycophorin A (N-out) right. With predicted helices glycophorin A goes wrong, because the signal peptide counts as a helix and flips the alternation. Aquaporin-1 is predicted N-out with five helices; it really has six TM helices with both ends in the cytoplasm, so one missing helix is enough to put the loops on the wrong sides. The β2-adrenergic receptor keeps its N-terminus outside with six helices, but its C-terminus is then placed outside, which is wrong.

Step 4 of 10

β-barrels hide from hydropathy plots

Outer membrane proteins of bacteria, mitochondria and chloroplasts cross the membrane as β-strands that close into a barrel. A strand only needs 7–10 residues to cross, and its side chains point alternately out into the lipid and into the barrel's interior. So the sequence alternates hydrophobic and polar, and a 19-residue average stays low. Instead, measure the hydrophobic moment at 180°: treat each residue's hydropathy as a vector pointing 180° from its neighbour's, add them up over a short window, and divide by the window length. Strict alternation gives a large moment.

What does the plot show, and what doesn't it?

For OmpA the 19-window hydropathy only rises above 1.6 over the signal peptide, so a TM helix scan would call OmpA a soluble protein with a signal peptide. The moment is higher in the strands on average, but it isn't a clean detector: soluble β-sheets (look at H-Ras) and parts of the periplasmic domain also alternate. Dedicated β-barrel predictors (DeepTMHMM, earlier PRED-TMBB and BOMP) combine many such signals with evolutionary information.

Step 5 of 10

From sequence to a 3D model in a membrane

AlphaFold models of membrane proteins are often good for the fold, because the PDB holds many membrane protein structures. What the model can't give you is the membrane. Place it yourself and check that the result makes physical sense.

A workflow

  1. Topology first. Run DeepTMHMM (or Phobius if you suspect a signal peptide). Compare with the UniProt "Topology" section, which is often based on experiments.
  2. Get a model. Download it from the AlphaFold DB or run ColabFold / AlphaFold Server. Check pLDDT as usual. Loops on the membrane surface are often less confident.
  3. Place the membrane. Upload the model to the PPM server, which finds the orientation and hydrophobic thickness that minimise the transfer energy. For known structures the OPM database already has the answer, and MemProtMD has coarse-grained simulations of PDB structures in a bilayer.
  4. Check it. The hydrophobic belt should be about 30 Å thick. Aromatic residues (Trp, Tyr) should cluster at the interfaces and K/R on the cytoplasmic side. The predicted TM segments should cross the slab and the loops should stay out.
  5. Multimers. Many membrane proteins are oligomers (aquaporins are tetramers). Submit the right number of copies, or you get a monomer with an exposed, hydrophobic interface.

Step 6 of 10

The heptad repeat on a helical wheel

An α-helix has 3.6 residues per turn (100° per residue). In a coiled coil the helices wrap around each other, so the repeat becomes 3.5 residues per turn: seven residues in two turns. The seven positions are called a b c d e f g. Positions a and d form a hydrophobic stripe that packs into the core, e and g flank the core and often form salt bridges between helices, and b, c, f face the solvent. Pick the register that puts the hydrophobic residues on the a/d face.

The wheel draws residues at 100° steps. The heptad letters assume 3.5 residues per turn, so the a and d residues drift by 20° per heptad on this wheel. In the real supercoil that drift is taken up by the helices winding around each other.

What's the register of GCN4-p1?

Residue 1 (Arg) is at g. That puts M2, V9, N16, V23, V30 at a and L5, L12, L19, L26 (and R33) at d: the leucines every seven residues gave the "leucine zipper" its name. The Asn at position 16 (an a position) is buried in the core and makes the parallel dimer the preferred state.

Step 7 of 10

Scan a sequence for coiled coils

COILS (Lupas et al., 1991) slides a 28-residue window (four heptads) along the sequence, scores every register with a position-specific table, and keeps the best score per residue. This page uses a simplified, transparent version with made-up weights, not the published matrices: a/d score 2 for L I V M F Y; e/g score 1 for E K R D (0.5 for Q N S T H A); b/c/f score 1 for polar or small residues; Pro scores −1 anywhere. A perfect heptad scores 9, and the window score is scaled to 0–1.

How good is this toy method?

It separates the examples here: GCN4 and most of tropomyosin score 0.8–0.9, while bacteriorhodopsin, OmpA and H-Ras stay below 0.75 (H-Ras comes closest, at 0.72). It misses the first 29 and residues 120–154 of tropomyosin, which is a coiled coil along its whole length but has alanines and other small residues in its core. Real tools use statistics from thousands of coiled coils (COILS, Marcoil) or deep learning on language-model embeddings (DeepCoil, CoCoNat).

Step 8 of 10

Dimer, trimer or tetramer? The core decides

Harbury, Zhang, Kim and Alber (1993) changed only the core of GCN4-p1. They replaced the a residues (positions 9, 16, 23, 30) and the d residues (5, 12, 19, 26) and named each peptide after its a and d residues. Changing eight core residues switched the oligomer state, because β-branched (Ile, Val) and unbranched (Leu) side chains pack differently at a and d.

Rules of thumb: β-branched a with Leu d → dimer; Ile at both a and d → trimer; Leu a with Ile d → tetramer; a buried Asn at a favours a dimer. They work for short GCN4-like zippers. Longer, natural coiled coils also depend on the e/g residues, length and context.

Try this with AlphaFold. Predict GCN4-pLI in AlphaFold Server or ColabFold (AlphaFold-Multimer) three times, with 2, 3 and 4 copies of the chain. Record ipTM and pTM for each run and compare. Then do the same for GCN4-p1. Does the highest ipTM pick the experimental state? Note the problem: AlphaFold never refuses the number of copies you give it; it builds a plausible bundle for each, and you have to compare.

What to look for

There is no single right number to report here: record what you get. A helpful habit is to look at ipTM and at the PAE between chains. If all three runs look equally confident, the model is not telling you the oligomer state, and you need experiments (size-exclusion chromatography with light scattering, analytical ultracentrifugation or crystallography).
