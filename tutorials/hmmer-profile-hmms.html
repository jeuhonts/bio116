Bio 116 · Tutorial · Profile hidden Markov models

Profile HMMs and HMMER

BLAST compares one sequence with another. HMMER compares a sequence with a whole family. It turns a multiple alignment into a probabilistic model that knows, position by position, which residues are allowed and where gaps are likely. In this tutorial you build a profile HMM from four animal globins, score sequences with it using the Viterbi and Forward algorithms, and learn to run HMMER and read its output.

  • About 55 minutes
  • Follows: Inside BLAST (PSI-BLAST), PAM and BLOSUM
  • All tools run in your browser

Six real nodes of the globin model you will build, around the proximal histidine F8 (β His92). Squares are match states, showing their most likely residues. Diamonds are insert states and circles are delete states. Arrow thickness shows transition probability.

By the end you should be able to

  • Describe the match, insert and delete states of a profile HMM and what their parameters mean.
  • Explain how hmmbuild turns a multiple alignment into emission and transition probabilities.
  • Contrast the Viterbi and Forward algorithms and explain why HMMER3 scores with Forward.
  • Interpret HMMER bit scores and E-values, including why E depends on database size.
  • Choose among hmmsearch, hmmscan, phmmer and jackhmmer, and read their output.

Part 1 · How profile HMMs work

1

Why profile HMMs?

In the BLAST tutorial, PSI-BLAST built a position-specific scoring matrix (PSSM) from its hits. That was already a profile: a different set of scores for every position of the family. But a PSSM still uses one gap penalty everywhere, and a family does not. Globins tolerate insertions and deletions between helices but almost never inside the heme pocket. Hemoglobin α lacks the whole D helix.

A profile hidden Markov model (Krogh et al. 1994; Eddy 1998) makes both residues and gaps position-specific, and it is a full probability model. Every score is a log-odds ratio of two probabilities, "this sequence came from the family model" versus "it came from random background", so scores can be interpreted and added in a principled way. HMMER (Sean Eddy's lab) is the standard implementation and powers Pfam and InterPro.

2

The model: states, emissions, transitions

A hidden Markov model generates sequences by walking through states. At each state it may emit a residue, then it transitions to a next state. We see the residues but not the path, which is why the states are "hidden". A profile HMM has one node per consensus column of the family alignment, and each node has three states:

  • Match Mk emits a residue from column k's own distribution: in the F8 node, almost always histidine.
  • Insert Ik emits extra residues between columns k and k+1, using background frequencies. The self-loop Ik → Ik lets insertions have any length.
  • Delete Dk emits nothing. It skips column k, as hemoglobin α does across the D helix.

HMMER's version, called Plan7, adds flanking states (N, C and J) that absorb unrelated sequence before, after and between matches. That lets a model match one part of a long protein, or match several times for repeated domains. In local mode, which HMMER uses for searches, an alignment may begin and end at any node.

Building a model: hmmbuild

hmmbuild reads a multiple alignment and estimates every probability. Below are human β- and α-globin and human and sperm whale myoglobin, aligned. Uppercase columns and dashes are the 146 consensus columns that become match states; here they are the β-globin positions. Lowercase letters and dots are insert columns.

Alignment → model Pick a window to see its states

The logo above is how HMM tools picture a model. At each match state, letter height is the residue's probability times the state's information content, in bits. Tall single letters are conserved positions, like the F8 histidine. Short stacks of many letters are variable positions. The bar under each column is that node's delete-open probability, Mk → Dk+1. It rises at the nodes where a deletion starts, such as where α-globin's D-helix deletion begins (window β 40–63).

Emissions e(k,a) = (α·f(k,a) + β·g(k,a)) / (α + β) f = observed frequency in column k, g = BLOSUM62-based pseudocounts, α = sequences − 1 Transitions t(k, X→Y) = (count(X→Y) + prior) / (count(X→·) + priors) Scores bits = log₂( e(k,a) / p(a) ) for emissions, log₂ t for transitions

Like hmmbuild, this page weights sequences with Henikoff position-based weights, so near-duplicates such as the two myoglobins count less. The real hmmbuild does two more things that this page simplifies. It uses Dirichlet mixture priors (Sjölander et al. 1996) instead of the BLOSUM62 pseudocounts here. And it lowers the effective number of sequences by entropy weighting, so the priors keep more influence. The idea is the same: observed counts, softened by prior knowledge of how amino acids substitute.

4

Scoring: Viterbi and Forward

Given a model and a sequence, there are two natural questions. What is the single most probable path of states that explains the sequence? And what is the total probability of the sequence, summed over every path? Both are dynamic programming, just like Smith–Waterman, but with three matrices (M, I, D) and position-specific transitions.

Viterbi V_M(i,k) = e(k,xᵢ) · max{ entry, V_M(i−1,k−1)·t(MM), V_I(i−1,k−1)·t(IM), V_D(i−1,k−1)·t(DM) } Forward F_M(i,k) = e(k,xᵢ) · sum{ entry, F_M(i−1,k−1)·t(MM), F_I(i−1,k−1)·t(IM), F_D(i−1,k−1)·t(DM) } (similar recurrences for I and D; e is emission odds against background; score = log₂ of the result)

Viterbi gives an alignment, the one HMMER displays. Forward gives a better score. A true but distant homolog often has many roughly equal ways to align: a shifted gap here, an alternative helix boundary there. Viterbi counts only the best one. Forward adds up the evidence from all of them. HMMER3 was the first version to score every hit with Forward (Eddy 2011), and it gained sensitivity by doing so.

5

From bit scores to E-values

HMMER reports scores in bits. A score of S bits means the sequence is 2S times more likely under the family model than under the background model. For HMMER3's Forward scores, Eddy (2008) showed that the tail of random scores falls off as an exponential with slope λ = ln 2. In words, each extra bit halves the chance of reaching that score at random. HMMER measures one offset τ per model by scoring simulated random sequences, then:

P(S ≥ x) ≈ 2^−(x − τ) E-value = Z × P Z = number of sequences searched

Note the difference from BLAST. HMMER's E-value multiplies by the number of sequences in the database, not residues. The mini search below calibrates τ separately for each target by scoring shuffled copies of it. Shuffles keep the target's length and amino-acid composition, so a sequence cannot score well just by being rich in the residues the model likes. HMMER handles composition with its own bias correction, reported in its output.

What to notice

  • Viterbi vs Forward. For the four training globins the two scores are nearly equal, because one path dominates. For leghemoglobin, Forward is several bits higher than Viterbi. Its evidence is spread over many weak alignments.
  • Composition matters. Leghemoglobin's Forward score sits well above the decoys'. But its shuffled copies also score high, because it is rich in the alanines, lysines and leucines that helical globins favour. Against its own shuffles it is not significant.
  • What you train on decides what you find. Untick both myoglobins and train on α and β only. Myoglobin scores fall from about 300 bits to 40–47 bits, still significant. Leghemoglobin rises to 18.6 bits with E ≈ 5 × 10⁻⁴, so this hemoglobin model finds it. In this data leghemoglobin resembles the hemoglobins slightly more than the myoglobins. Two near-identical myoglobins in the training set pull the model toward myoglobin.
  • Weighting helps, a little. With all four sequences, switch sequence weights to None. Leghemoglobin's E-value goes from 0.057 to 0.12. Weighting stops the myoglobin pair from counting twice, but it cannot add information the alignment does not have.
  • Scale matters. Set Z to Swiss-Prot size. Even the hemoglobin model's leghemoglobin hit becomes E ≈ 30. A four-sequence model is too narrow to find plant globins in a real database.

This is why real family models are built from large, diverse seed alignments. Pfam's globin family, PF00042, is trained on globins from across the tree of life. To see the difference, submit soybean leghemoglobin (UniProt P02238) to hmmscan against Pfam on the EBI HMMER website, and compare its E-value with what this four-sequence model manages.

7

How HMMER3 is fast

Forward is expensive. For each target it fills three matrices and sums over every path. Older HMMER versions were about 100 times slower than BLAST. HMMER3 closed the gap with a pipeline of filters (Eddy 2011). Most sequences are thrown out by a cheap test, and only survivors reach the expensive one. The filters are vectorized with SIMD instructions that update many cells at once.

  1. 1MSV filterMultiple ungapped segments, much like BLAST seeds, scored for the whole model at once. Passes about 2% of random sequences (P ≤ 0.02).
  2. 2Viterbi filterFull gapped Viterbi in reduced precision. Passes about 0.1% (P ≤ 10⁻³).
  3. 3ForwardThe real score. Passes P ≤ 10⁻⁵, then domains are defined and aligned with posterior probabilities.

Filters are tuned so real homologs almost always survive. The thresholds are options (--F1, --F2, --F3), and --max turns all filters off for maximum sensitivity at a large cost in time.

Reading HMMER output

hmmsearch and hmmscan print two levels of results. First is one line per sequence, using the whole-sequence score. Then comes one block per domain, each a separate match of the model within that sequence. A protein with three copies of a repeat has one sequence line and three domain lines.

FieldLevelMeaning
E-valuesequenceExpected number of random sequences in a database of size Z scoring this well. Your main filter.
scorebothBit score, after bias correction. Independent of database size.
biasbothBits removed for biased composition. If bias is close to the raw score, the hit may be only a composition match.
exp / NsequenceExpected and reported number of domains in this sequence.
c-EvaluedomainConditional E-value: significance of this domain given that the sequence already matched. Use it to judge extra domains of a real hit.
i-EvaluedomainIndependent E-value: significance if this domain were the only match in the whole search. The stricter number.
hmm from/todomainWhich model nodes aligned. [] means both ends of the model were reached, .. means neither.
ali / env from/todomainAligned region of the target, and the wider envelope where the domain probably lies. Use the envelope as the domain's boundaries.
accdomainMean posterior probability of the aligned residues (0–1). Near 1 means the alignment is well determined.
PP linealignmentPosterior probability of each aligned residue: 0–9 means 0–95%, * means over 95%. Low PP flags regions to distrust.

Rules of thumb

  • HMMER reports hits with E ≤ 10 but marks as included (!) only those with E ≤ 0.01. Treat unincluded hits (?) as leads, not results.
  • For Pfam, use the curated gathering thresholds with --cut_ga. Each family's curators chose a bit-score cutoff with no known false positives.
  • A significant sequence E-value with every individual domain weak means the evidence is spread over several weak domains, often repeats. Check the domain table.
  • High bias relative to score, or low acc, is a warning even when the E-value looks good.

Check yourself

1. What does a profile HMM capture that a PSSM does not?

Position-specific gap probabilities. Insert and delete transitions are estimated for every node, so the model expects gaps between helices and penalizes them inside conserved cores. A PSSM uses one gap penalty everywhere.

2. Why is an insert state's emission distribution usually set to background?

Inserted residues vary between family members and are not aligned to each other, so there is no column-specific signal to learn. Using background makes inserted residues score zero bits; the insert's cost comes only from its transitions.

3. Viterbi gives leghemoglobin a low score, but Forward gives it several bits more. Why?

Viterbi keeps only the single best path. For a distant homolog, many different paths each explain part of the sequence about equally well. Forward sums their probabilities, so it collects evidence the single best path leaves out.

4. The same hit has E = 0.06 with Z = 9 and E ≈ 3,600 with Z = 570,000. What changed?

Only the number of sequences searched. The P-value per sequence is the same. Searching more sequences gives chance more tries, so the expected number of equally good random hits rises in proportion.

5. You want to know which Pfam domains a new protein contains. Which program, and which threshold option?

hmmscan against Pfam-A (after hmmpress), with --cut_ga to apply Pfam's curated gathering thresholds.

6. Training on α and β only, myoglobin scores drop but leghemoglobin's rises. What does this tell you about building models?

A model recognizes the variation it was trained on. Remove the myoglobins and it loses some of its grip on myoglobins. Add two near-identical myoglobins and the model tilts toward them, even with sequence weighting. Good family models start from diverse, carefully curated seed alignments that sample the whole family evenly, which is what Pfam's curators build.
