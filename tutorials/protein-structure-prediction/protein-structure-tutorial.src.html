From sequence to structure: Ras and haemoglobin

You have a protein sequence and want to know its 3D structure. This tutorial takes two well-studied proteins, H-Ras and human haemoglobin, and predicts their structure with methods from the 1970s to today. Because both structures are known experimentally, you can see on the 3D structure exactly which parts each method got right. Everything on this page runs in your browser, even offline.

Background in two minutes

Anfinsen showed that for many small proteins the sequence alone determines the fold, so structure should be computable. Levinthal pointed out that a 100-residue chain has about 3100 ≈ 1048 conformations, so neither the protein nor a computer can search them all. Prediction methods are shortcuts built from what we already know about proteins:

1970sChou–Fasman and GOR: residue propensities for helix and strand. Q3 ≈ 50–65%.
1988Neural networks learn the rules from known structures (Qian & Sejnowski). Q3 ≈ 64%.
1990sEvolutionary profiles (PHD, PSIPRED), homology modelling and threading.
2010sCo-evolution: residues that mutate together are usually in contact.
2020AlphaFold2 reaches near-experimental accuracy in CASP14.
2022–24ESMFold (language model, no MSA); AlphaFold3 and Boltz add ligands, DNA and RNA.
Step 1 of 8

Meet the two proteins

Both proteins are famous, both have been solved many times, and they look nothing alike. That makes them good test cases: a method has to get β-strands right for Ras and long helices right for haemoglobin. Pick the protein to work with; the choice carries through every step.

GTPase · alpha/beta fold · UniProt P01112

H-Ras

A molecular switch: active when bound to GTP, inactive with GDP. Mutations at G12, G13 or Q61 lock it "on" and drive many cancers. 189 residues: a folded G domain (1–166) with a six-stranded β-sheet surrounded by five helices, plus a flexible tail that anchors it to the membrane.

Reference structure: PDB 1CRR, the NMR structure of wild-type H-Ras·GDP (residues 1–166, model 1 of 20).

Oxygen carrier · all-alpha globin fold · UniProt P68871 / P69905

Haemoglobin

Carries O2 in red blood cells as a tetramer of two α chains (141 residues) and two β chains (146 residues), each holding a haem. Each chain folds into eight helices (A–H) with no β-strands at all. The sickle-cell mutation changes β Glu6 to Val.

Reference structure: PDB 4HHB, human deoxyhaemoglobin by X-ray crystallography (Fermi et al. 1984).

Step 2 of 8

What the sequence alone tells you

Before building a 3D model, simple statistics already hint at the structure: whether the protein is soluble or sits in a membrane, and how much of it is helix or strand.

Protein

Hydropathy plot (Kyte–Doolittle)

Each point is the average hydrophobicity of a window centred on that residue. A 19-residue window above 1.6 suggests a helix that spans the membrane.

Secondary structure: Chou–Fasman

Average each residue's helix propensity (Pα) and strand propensity (Pβ) over a window. Call helix if ⟨Pα⟩ passes its threshold and beats ⟨Pβ⟩, strand if ⟨Pβ⟩ passes its threshold, otherwise coil. Then drop helices shorter than 4 and strands shorter than 3. The DSSP row is the secondary structure of the experimental structure; Q3 is the fraction of residues predicted correctly.

Discussion

For H-Ras the method gets about 60% right. For haemoglobin β it gets under 40%, and it calls about a quarter of the chain β-strand even though haemoglobin has no strands at all. Residues such as Val, Leu, Phe and Tyr have high strand propensities, and haemoglobin's buried helix faces are full of them. Chou–Fasman only looks at a small window of residues, so it can't tell a hydrophobic helix face from a strand.

Tuning the thresholds to fix one protein breaks the other: that is overfitting. The methods in the next step learn from thousands of proteins instead.

Step 3 of 8

Neural networks: learning the rules from data

Chou and Fasman tabulated their propensities by hand from a few dozen known structures. In 1988, Qian and Sejnowski instead let a neural network learn the rules. It looks at a window of residues around the one being predicted and outputs a probability for helix, strand and coil. Below you can train that kind of network in your browser on 5,517 real proteins and test it on the standard CB513 benchmark. Relatives of Ras and haemoglobin are removed from the training data, so the network has never seen either protein family.

How the network sees a residue

Each residue in the window becomes a column of 22 inputs: 20 amino acids, one for unknown (X) and one for "past the end of the chain". Exactly one input per column is on (one-hot encoding). The hidden units combine them, and the three outputs are turned into probabilities (softmax). Training nudges every weight to make the correct answer a little more likely (gradient descent). Move the slider to look at any residue of the selected protein.

What you should see

Context matters. One residue on its own gives about 48%, barely better than always guessing "coil" (43% of CB513). A window of 13–17 gives about 62–65%.

Haemoglobin improves the most. Chou–Fasman gets under 40% of haemoglobin β right. The network typically gets 50–70%, depending on the settings and the run: it has learned from thousands of helices that a hydrophobic residue every three or four positions usually means a helix face, not a strand. Scores for a single protein jump around from run to run; the CB513 score is the reliable one.

The model size is not the bottleneck. Hidden units add only a point or two. A single sequence simply doesn't carry much more information about local structure, so single-sequence methods level off around 65%.

Too little data means overfitting. With 50 proteins the training accuracy keeps climbing while the test accuracy stalls, and the gap between them grows.

A second network cleans up the output. It reads the first network's probabilities over 17 neighbouring residues and learns that helices come in runs, which removes isolated one-residue "helices" and adds about a point of Q3. PHD (1993) used this cascade.

How evolution broke the 65% barrier

In 1993, Rost and Sander's PHD gave the same kind of network a profile instead of a single sequence: for each position, how often each amino acid appears at that position across related proteins in a multiple sequence alignment. A position that is always hydrophobic in homologues is probably buried. A strand's alternating pattern shows up much more clearly across 100 sequences than in one.

PSIPRED (1999) built the profiles with PSI-BLAST and reached about 76%. Deep networks with profiles now reach about 85% on CB513. The practical ceiling is roughly 88–90%, because DSSP itself assigns slightly different states to different structures of the same protein. The same idea, evolution as data, is what powers AlphaFold's MSA input in step 5.

Solid green bars were measured on this page, on CB513. Grey bars are published values measured on different test sets, so compare them loosely.

What to look for

Chou–Fasman paints whole haemoglobin helices red because it calls them strands (the "wrongly called strand" count). The network still calls a few short strands and cuts several helices short, but far fewer residues are wrongly called strand. Retrain it in step 3 with other settings and watch the colours change. In H-Ras, look at the β-hairpin formed by β2 and β3 (residues 37–58): both methods get only part of it. What makes a strand a strand is its partner strand, which can be far away in the sequence, so local methods find strands harder than helices.

For haemoglobin, the faint grey chains are the other three subunits of the tetramer and the red spheres are the haem irons. A single-chain prediction knows nothing about them.

What the network does

MSA and co-evolution. If residue i mutates and residue j compensates across many related species, the two are probably in contact. AlphaFold2's Evoformer reasons over the alignment and a residue-pair map through 48 blocks. Globins and Ras-family GTPases both have thousands of relatives, so their alignments are deep.

Structure module. Each residue is treated as a rigid frame that is rotated and moved into place. The model is recycled three times and outputs coordinates together with pLDDT and PAE. AlphaFold3 replaces this step with diffusion, which also lets it place ligands such as haem or GTP.

What to expect. An AlphaFold2 model of H-Ras should put nearly all of residues 1–166 within 2 Å of the experiment. The C-terminal tail (167–189) has no experimental coordinates here and very low pLDDT. Because 1CRR is an NMR structure, a few loops can differ by more than 2 Å even when the model is good. For a haemoglobin chain, expect high pLDDT across all eight helices.

The demo uses the experimental α chain as a "model" of the β chain. They are 43% identical, which is what homology modelling relies on: 73% of β lands within 2 Å. The unmatched stretch around β residues 45–55 is where α chains are shorter: they lack the D helix. PAE (ColabFold, AlphaFold DB) adds how confident the model is about the relative position of two parts.

Step 7 of 8

What predictors can't tell you

Both proteins work through details that a single static model of one chain misses.

Ras: two states

Ras switches between inactive GDP-bound and active GTP-bound states. The changes are concentrated in switch I (≈30–38) and switch II (≈59–76). AlphaFold2 gives you only one of them.

Ras: point mutations

Predict G12V: the model looks almost the same as wild type. The mutation is oncogenic because it blocks GTP hydrolysis, not because it changes the fold.

Haemoglobin: the tetramer and the haem

A single β chain folds correctly on its own in a prediction, but haemoglobin only works as α₂β₂ with four haem groups. AlphaFold2 has no haem; AlphaFold3 can add it and build the tetramer.

Haemoglobin: allostery and sickle cell

Oxygen binding shifts the tetramer from the T state (4HHB) to the R state. Sickle-cell haemoglobin (β E6V) folds normally; the disease comes from deoxy HbS tetramers sticking together into fibres, which no single-chain model shows.
