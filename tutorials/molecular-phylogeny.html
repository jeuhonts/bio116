Globin Family Trees

A multiple alignment says which residues are homologous. A phylogenetic tree says how the sequences are related: which ones share a more recent common ancestor, and how much change separates them. This tutorial builds trees from the globin alignment of the last tutorial using distances, neighbor-joining, parsimony and the bootstrap. It then introduces the likelihood and Bayesian methods used in practice.

  • About 55 minutes
  • Follows: Aligning Many Globins
  • All tools run in your browser

By the end you should be able to

  • Read a phylogenetic tree: topology, branch lengths, root, clades and Newick format.
  • Tell orthologs from paralogs, and a gene tree from a species tree.
  • Explain why observed differences must be corrected for multiple substitutions.
  • Build a tree by neighbor-joining and score trees by parsimony.
  • Interpret bootstrap support, and know when to use maximum likelihood or Bayesian methods.

Part 1 · Trees and what they claim

1

Reading a tree

  • Leaves (tips) are the sequences you have. Internal nodes are inferred ancestors that you never observe.
  • Topology is the branching pattern. Rotating two branches around a node does not change it.
  • Branch length is the estimated amount of change, usually substitutions per site. Only horizontal distance counts; vertical spacing is just for layout.
  • A clade is an ancestor and all of its descendants. On the tree above, human and whale myoglobin form a clade.
  • Most methods produce an unrooted tree: relationships without a direction of time. To root it, add an outgroup known to have branched off first (here leghemoglobin), or use the midpoint of the longest path.

Gene trees are not species trees

A tree of sequences is a gene tree. Its nodes can be two kinds of event (Fitch 1970):

  • Speciation (S): a population splits into two species, each carrying a copy of the gene. The copies are orthologs. Human and sperm whale myoglobin are orthologs, separated by the split between the primate and whale lineages roughly 90–100 million years ago.
  • Duplication (D): a gene is copied within one genome, and the copies evolve separately. They are paralogs. Hemoglobin α and β are paralogs: both are in your genome. They arose by duplication about 450 million years ago in the ancestor of jawed vertebrates. Myoglobin and hemoglobin are paralogs from an even older split, probably during the genome duplications of early vertebrates.

This is why the globin tree does not match the tree of life. Human α-globin is more closely related to whale α-globin than to human β-globin. Mixing orthologs and paralogs without knowing it is one of the most common mistakes in phylogenetics.

3

How many trees are there?

Each new sequence can be attached to any branch of the existing tree, so the number of possible trees grows faster than exponentially. With n sequences there are (2n − 5)!! unrooted binary trees, the product 3 × 5 × 7 × … × (2n − 5).

Five globins have 15 unrooted trees, few enough to check every one, as the parsimony section does. For real data sets, programs search tree space heuristically.

Part 2 · Building trees

4

From alignment to distances

The simplest measure is the p-distance: the fraction of aligned sites where two sequences differ. It underestimates real change. A site can change twice, or change and change back, and still show one difference or none. As sequences diverge, p levels off (saturates) while the true number of substitutions keeps growing. Correction formulas estimate the hidden changes:

p-distance d = p Poisson d = −ln(1 − p) each site changes at the same rate Kimura protein d = −ln(1 − p − 0.2 p²) an empirical fit for amino acids (Kimura 1983); undefined when p is above about 0.854
Distances from the globin alignment Sites where both sequences have a residue

For human and whale myoglobin (p ≈ 0.16) the corrections barely matter. For leghemoglobin against the animal globins (p ≈ 0.82), the Kimura distance is several times p. Without correction, the long branches in the tree would be far too short.

5

Neighbor-joining, step by step

Neighbor-joining (Saitou & Nei 1987) builds a tree by repeatedly joining two nodes. It does not simply join the closest pair. It joins the pair that minimizes Q, which also accounts for how far each node is from all the others. A fast-evolving sequence then does not look close to everything just because its branch is long.

Q(i,j) = (n − 2)·d(i,j) − Σₖ d(i,k) − Σₖ d(j,k) join the pair with the smallest Q into a new node u: branch i–u = d(i,j)/2 + (Σₖ d(i,k) − Σₖ d(j,k)) / (2(n − 2)) branch j–u = d(i,j) − branch i–u d(u,k) = (d(i,k) + d(j,k) − d(i,j)) / 2 repeat until three nodes remain, then join them to one centre

Switch the tree method to UPGMA, the clustering method that built the guide tree in the last tutorial. It gives the same groups here but forces every leaf to the same distance from the root. That is a molecular clock: equal rates in all lineages. Real proteins rarely keep a clock, so UPGMA is a poor phylogeny method, however useful it is for guide trees. Neighbor-joining lets branch lengths differ.

6

Maximum parsimony

Parsimony skips distances and works column by column. The best tree is the one that explains the alignment with the fewest substitutions. For one column on one tree, the Fitch (1971) algorithm counts the minimum number of changes. Starting at the tips, each ancestor gets the intersection of its children's residue sets if that is not empty, and otherwise their union plus one change. Add up all columns and you have the tree's length.

Only parsimony-informative columns can prefer one tree over another: at least two residue types that each appear in at least two sequences. With five sequences we can score all 15 trees.

One column at a time

The best tree, the same one neighbor-joining found, wins by a single step over tree #2. In tree #2, α-globin groups with the myoglobins and β-globin branches off next to leghemoglobin. Seen unrooted, it pairs β-globin with leghemoglobin. Step through the informative columns and you will find some that support each. Parsimony is intuitive and needs no model. But it has no correction for multiple changes, so it can be misled when some branches are much longer than others (see Pitfalls).

7

How sure are we? The bootstrap

A tree is an estimate from a finite alignment. Felsenstein's (1985) bootstrap asks how much each clade depends on the particular columns you happened to have. Build a new alignment of the same length by sampling columns at random with replacement, rebuild the tree, and repeat hundreds of times. A clade's bootstrap support is the percentage of replicate trees that contain it.

The myoglobin pair is recovered in nearly every replicate. The hemoglobin pair is weaker. In a sizeable fraction of replicates, β-globin pairs with leghemoglobin instead, which appears here as the group (HBA, MBh, MBw) because the tree is rooted on leghemoglobin. A common rule of thumb treats standard bootstrap values of 70% or more as reasonable support. Values like these are a warning: the data barely resolve that part of the tree.

8

Maximum likelihood and Bayesian trees

The methods used in current research are model-based. A substitution model gives the probability of each amino acid changing into each other one along a branch of given length. Protein models such as JTT, WAG and LG are estimated from large alignment databases, like the PAM and BLOSUM matrices. A +G (gamma) term lets rates vary across sites, because some sites barely change while others change often.

Maximum likelihood (ML)

  • Finds the tree and branch lengths that make the observed alignment most probable under the model (Felsenstein 1981).
  • Uses every column and corrects for multiple changes through the model.
  • Support: bootstrap, or fast approximations (UFBoot, SH-aLRT).
  • Programs: IQ-TREE, RAxML-NG, PhyML.

Bayesian inference

  • Samples trees in proportion to their posterior probability, combining the likelihood with prior beliefs.
  • Uses Markov chain Monte Carlo; you must check that runs have converged.
  • Support: posterior probability of each clade.
  • Programs: MrBayes, BEAST (for dated trees).

Part 3 · Doing it for real

9

Building real trees

A typical workflow:

  1. Collect homologs (BLAST or HMMER) and decide whether you want orthologs, paralogs, or both.
  2. Align them (Clustal Omega, MAFFT, MUSCLE), then check the alignment.
  3. Trim unreliable columns (trimAl).
  4. Choose a substitution model. IQ-TREE's ModelFinder does this automatically.
  5. Build an ML tree with branch support.
  6. Root it with an outgroup, then view and annotate it in iTOL, FigTree or MEGA.
# IQ-TREE 2: choose the best model, build an ML tree, 1000 ultrafast bootstraps + SH-aLRT iqtree2 -s globins_aligned.fasta -m MFP -B 1000 -alrt 1000 # Output: globins_aligned.fasta.treefile (Newick) and .iqtree (report, chosen model)

MEGA does the same steps (alignment, models, NJ, ML, bootstrap) in a point-and-click program, which is convenient for a first analysis. Ultrafast bootstrap values are not on the same scale as standard bootstrap. The IQ-TREE documentation recommends trusting clades with UFBoot of 95% or more.

Pitfalls

  • Long-branch attraction. Two fast-evolving or distant sequences share many changes by chance, and methods without a good model (especially parsimony) group them together (Felsenstein 1978). Leghemoglobin's long branch tugging β-globin toward it in the parsimony and bootstrap results above is a small example. Remedies: better models, more taxa that break up long branches, removing saturated sites.
  • Paralogs mistaken for orthologs. A tree of genes from different species can show gene duplications, not species relationships.
  • Wrong or missing root. Unrooted trees say nothing about which group is older. An outgroup that is too distant can root the tree in the wrong place.
  • Alignment errors. Trees inherit every alignment mistake. Trim unreliable regions and check that the tree holds up.
  • Too few sites or taxa. Five sequences and about 150 columns resolve some clades well and others poorly, as the bootstrap shows.
11

Check yourself

1. Human α-globin and human β-globin: orthologs or paralogs? Human and whale myoglobin?

α and β are paralogs: they arose by gene duplication and both exist in one genome. Human and whale myoglobin are orthologs: they diverged when the species did.

2. Why is the Kimura distance for leghemoglobin vs myoglobin much larger than its p-distance?

At p ≈ 0.8, many sites have changed more than once, and some differences hide several changes. The correction estimates those hidden substitutions. At low p (myoglobin pair) the correction is tiny.

3. Neighbor-joining joins the pair minimizing Q rather than the closest pair. What problem does this solve?

A sequence with a long branch is far from everything, and one with a short branch is close to everything. Q subtracts each node's total distance to all others, so it joins true neighbors even when rates differ between lineages.

4. What does UPGMA assume that neighbor-joining does not?

A molecular clock: every lineage evolves at the same rate, so all tips are the same distance from the root. When rates differ, UPGMA can give the wrong topology.

5. A clade has 64% bootstrap support. What does that mean, and what doesn't it mean?

64% of trees built from resampled alignments contained the clade, so the data support it only weakly. It is not a 64% probability that the clade is true. Bootstrap values measure how consistently the data support a group, not its truth.

6. Why is parsimony vulnerable to long-branch attraction?

It counts the minimum number of changes and has no model of multiple substitutions. Two long branches accumulate many parallel changes by chance, and parsimony reads those as shared history. Model-based methods expect such coincidences on long branches and are less easily fooled.

7. How many unrooted trees are possible for 10 sequences?

(2·10 − 5)!! = 15!! = 3 × 5 × 7 × 9 × 11 × 13 × 15 = 2,027,025.
