PAM and BLOSUM

Every alignment score you have seen so far came from a substitution matrix. This tutorial opens the matrices up. You will build a small BLOSUM matrix from real globin sequences and extrapolate a PAM series step by step. Then you will see why each matrix is tuned for proteins at one particular evolutionary distance, and how to pick the right one.

  • About 45 minutes
  • Follows: Aligning the Globins, Inside BLAST
  • All tools run in your browser

  • Build a BLOSUM-style matrix from an aligned block, including sequence clustering.

  • Choose a protein or DNA matrix for a given search and justify the choice.
1

Why not just count identities?

The simplest scoring rewards identical residues and penalizes everything else. For DNA that is often fine. For proteins it throws away chemistry. Swapping leucine for isoleucine usually keeps a protein working; swapping tryptophan for glycine usually does not. Here is β-globin aligned to soybean leghemoglobin, two proteins about 20% identical, first with identity scoring and then with BLOSUM62.

Same pair, two scoring schemes Global alignment, gap 10 / 0.5, as in the first tutorial

With identity scoring, the proximal histidines that bind heme end up in different columns. BLOSUM62 puts them together, because it knows which mismatches are acceptable. The matrix carries information about protein evolution that identity scoring lacks. The rest of this tutorial is about where that information comes from.

2

Scores are log-odds

Every standard substitution matrix answers one question for each pair of residues a and b. How much more often do they appear aligned in real homologous proteins than they would pair up by chance?

s(a,b) = (1/λ) · ln( q(a,b) / (p(a) · p(b)) ) q(a,b) target frequency: how often a and b are aligned in true homologs p(a) background frequency of residue a λ scale. BLOSUM62 uses half-bits (λ = ln2 / 2), so a score of +2 means "twice as likely"

Adding scores along an alignment multiplies the odds, so the total score measures how much more likely the alignment is under "these are homologs" than under "these are random". Any useful matrix has three properties, which the table checks for you:

  • Negative expected score. Aligning random sequences must lose points on average, otherwise local alignments would grow without limit.
  • A recoverable λ. Given only the scores and background frequencies, λ is the unique positive number with Σ p(a)p(b)eλ·s(a,b) = 1. From λ you can recover the target frequencies the matrix implies.
  • Relative entropy H. The average information per aligned pair, in bits. High H suits close relatives and short alignments. Low H suits distant relatives, which need long alignments to score well.

All values are computed in your browser from the published matrices and the Robinson–Robinson background frequencies that NCBI BLAST uses. Henikoff and Henikoff's published H for BLOSUM62 (0.70 bits) used the block database's own frequencies, so it differs a little.

3

Building BLOSUM from blocks

Henikoff and Henikoff (1992) took about 2,000 blocks: short, gap-free stretches of aligned sequence from more than 500 protein families. They counted every aligned residue pair in every column and turned the counts into log-odds scores. Below is one real block of 22 columns: helix E of the five globins, including the distal histidine (highlighted).

Build a matrix from one block β-globin positions 57–78 and the matching columns of the other four

A dot means the pair never appears in this block, so its log-odds is −∞. The real BLOSUM62 needed thousands of blocks to see every pair often enough.

The four steps

1 Count pairs. In each column, count every pair of residues between sequences in different clusters. A cluster's members share one vote (weight 1/size each). 2 Frequencies. q(a,b) = count(a,b) / total pairs p(a) = q(a,a) + ½ Σ over b≠a of q(a,b) 3 Expected. e(a,a) = p(a)² e(a,b) = 2·p(a)·p(b) (a≠b: the pair can appear either way round) 4 Score. s(a,b) = round( 2 · log₂( q(a,b) / e(a,b) ) )

Clustering is where the number comes from. BLOSUM62 merges sequences that are at least 62% identical before counting, so near-duplicates do not swamp the counts. Human and whale myoglobin are about 90% identical in this block, so at 62% they merge into one cluster and their 22 near-identical pairs stop being counted. At 40%, α- and β-globin merge too. A high cutoff like BLOSUM80 keeps close relatives apart, so the matrix learns what close homologs look like. A low cutoff like BLOSUM45 counts only pairs between distant clusters. Change the clustering and watch the pair count fall from 220 to 132 to 66. H–H stays at +7 because every sequence has histidine in that column, but scores built from so few pairs, such as K–K, jump around. That instability is why the real matrices needed thousands of blocks.

4

Building PAM by extrapolation

Margaret Dayhoff's group built the first widely used matrices in the 1970s (Dayhoff, Schwartz & Orcutt 1978). Their approach was evolutionary rather than statistical:

  1. Collect alignments of very close relatives (at least 85% identical), where each difference is probably a single mutation. They found 1,572 accepted point mutations in 71 protein families. "Accepted" means natural selection let the change survive.
  2. Build a phylogenetic tree for each family and count how often each amino acid was replaced by each other one.
  3. Turn the counts into a mutation probability matrix M, scaled so that on average 1 residue in 100 changes. That is 1 PAM of evolution.
  4. Assume evolution is a Markov chain: every step uses the same M, and the past does not matter. Then the matrix for n PAMs is M multiplied by itself n times: Mn.
  5. Convert to log-odds: s(a,b) = 10 · log₁₀( Mn(a,b) / p(b) ), rounded to an integer.

PAM250 therefore means 250 accepted mutations per 100 residues. Many positions have changed more than once, so sequences at PAM250 are still about 20% identical, not 0%. Step 4 is the key idea and also the weak point. Errors in the 1-PAM matrix compound with every multiplication.

The same idea works for DNA, where it is simple enough to compute live. Below, 1 PAM has a 1% chance of change per base. Transitions (A↔G, C↔T) are κ times as likely as each transversion.

Extrapolate a DNA PAM matrix Kimura two-parameter model, equal base frequencies

Slide n upward. Identity falls toward 25%, the level of random DNA. The match reward shrinks and the mismatch penalty softens, because at long distances a mismatch is expected and says little. This is exactly why NCBI's BLAST documentation recommends different DNA scores for different targets (States, Gish & Altschul 1991). The table computes the ideal match : mismatch ratio for each target identity from the same log-odds formula.

5

PAM versus BLOSUM

PAM (Dayhoff 1978)

  • Counts substitutions on trees of close relatives (≥85% identical).
  • Builds one 1-PAM matrix, then extrapolates: PAM-n = Mn.
  • Number = evolutionary distance. Higher number = more distant proteins.
  • Assumes every site evolves the same way at every distance.
  • Built from about 1,500 mutations; small by modern standards.

BLOSUM (Henikoff 1992)

  • Counts pairs directly in conserved blocks at each level of divergence.
  • No extrapolation: each matrix comes from its own counts.
  • Number = clustering cutoff. Higher number = closer proteins.
  • Blocks are conserved regions, which suits database search.
  • Built from thousands of blocks; generally more sensitive in searches.

The two numbers run in opposite directions. PAM30 and BLOSUM80 are both for close relatives. PAM250 and BLOSUM45 are both for distant ones.

Choosing a matrix

A matrix is tuned for one distance. Using a shallow matrix (PAM30, BLOSUM90) on distant relatives rewards only near-identical stretches, so the alignment shrinks to a short fragment. Using a deep matrix (PAM250, BLOSUM45) on close relatives still works, but wastes information and scores less. Test this on the globins. Each row uses BLAST's default gap costs for that matrix.

The query-length rows follow NCBI's BLAST help. There are also specialized matrices: JTT and WAG are built for phylogenetic models, and some matrices are built only from transmembrane segments or from proteins with unusual composition. BLAST itself adjusts BLOSUM62 for each query–subject pair's amino-acid composition by default (compositional adjustment), because a matrix built for average proteins can mislead on biased ones.

Check yourself

1. Why must a substitution matrix have a negative expected score?

Local alignment keeps extending while the score grows. If random pairs scored positive on average, an alignment of two unrelated sequences would keep growing with length, and every local alignment would span the whole sequence. A negative expected score makes random alignments drift down, so high-scoring segments stand out.

2. BLOSUM62 and PAM250: which is for closer relatives, and what does each number mean?

BLOSUM62 is for closer relatives. Its number is the clustering cutoff: sequences ≥62% identical were merged before counting. PAM250's number is an evolutionary distance: 250 accepted point mutations per 100 residues, which leaves about 20% identity.

3. Why does BLOSUM cluster similar sequences before counting pairs?

Protein databases over-represent some families and species. Without clustering, a group of near-identical sequences (like human and whale myoglobin in the block) contributes many identical pairs, and the matrix learns mostly about that one close group. Clustering gives each group one vote, and the cutoff sets which evolutionary distances the counted pairs come from.

4. What assumption lets Dayhoff get PAM250 from PAM1, and why is it a weakness?

The Markov assumption: every PAM of evolution uses the same substitution probabilities, independent of history and position. Real proteins have sites under very different constraints, and small counting errors in PAM1 are magnified by 250 matrix multiplications.

5. You search with a 20-residue peptide using BLOSUM62 and find nothing significant. What would you change, and why?

Switch to PAM30. A 20-residue alignment has little room to accumulate score, so each position must carry more information (high relative entropy). PAM30 gives large rewards for identities and harsh penalties for mismatches, which suits short, near-identical matches.

6. Why are DNA searches for near-identical sequences scored +1/−3 rather than +1/−1?

If you expect 99% identity, a mismatch is rare in true matches and strong evidence against them, so it should cost far more than a match earns. The log-odds formula gives a ratio of about 1 : −3 at 99% identity and about 1 : −1 at 75%.
