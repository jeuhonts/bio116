Aligning Many Globins

Pairwise alignment compares two sequences. A multiple sequence alignment (MSA) lines up a whole family at once, so that every column holds residues that descend from one ancestral position. In this tutorial you build an MSA of the five course globins with the progressive method used by Clustal. You then test how the guide tree and gap costs change the result and learn to run and judge the modern tools.

  • About 50 minutes
  • Follows: all four earlier Bio 116 tutorials
  • All tools run in your browser

By the end you should be able to

  • Say what an MSA claims and list what MSAs are used for.

  • Explain why exact multiple alignment is computationally out of reach.
  • Carry out progressive alignment: distances, guide tree, profile merges.
  • Choose among Clustal Omega, MAFFT, MUSCLE and T-Coffee, and check an alignment critically.

Part 1 · How multiple alignment works

1

Why align many sequences?

A pairwise alignment is a hypothesis about two sequences. An MSA extends that hypothesis to a whole family. Each column claims that its residues are homologous, and each gap claims an insertion or deletion. With more sequences, patterns appear that no single pair can show. A column that stays histidine in hemoglobin, myoglobin and leghemoglobin is almost certainly functional.

MSAs are the input to most of what comes after this course:

  • Conserved residues: finding active sites, binding sites and structural cores.
  • Family models: PSSMs (PSI-BLAST) and profile HMMs (HMMER, Pfam) are built from MSAs.
  • Phylogenetics: every tree-building method treats each column as a character.
  • Structure prediction: AlphaFold2 and similar methods read co-varying columns in a deep MSA.
  • Variant interpretation: a disease mutation at a column conserved across species is more likely harmful.
2

Scoring an MSA

The most common objective is the sum-of-pairs (SP) score. Every MSA implies a pairwise alignment for each pair of its sequences: drop the other rows and any columns that become all-gap. Score each of those with a substitution matrix and gap costs, as in the first tutorial, and add them up. Five sequences give 10 pairs.

SP(MSA) = Σ over pairs (a,b) of score( alignment of a and b induced by the MSA ) Here: BLOSUM62, gap open 10, extend 0.5 (the pairwise defaults from the first tutorial)

SP has a known weakness. It treats all pairs as independent, so a family with many near-identical members counts the same evidence many times. Viewers such as Jalview and Clustal also print a per-column conservation line. * means every sequence has the identical residue. : means all residues fall in one strongly similar group, such as MILV or FYW. . means one weakly similar group. A blank means no pattern or a gap. You will see this line under every alignment on this page.

3

Why exact alignment is impossible

Needleman–Wunsch generalizes to N sequences. Replace the 2D matrix with an N-dimensional one. Each cell then looks back at 2N − 1 neighbors: every combination of "this sequence advances or has a gap". For globin-length sequences (L ≈ 150) the cost explodes:

Times assume a billion cell updates per second. Maximizing the SP score is NP-hard (Wang & Jiang 1994), so no clever shortcut is expected to make exact alignment practical. Every real MSA program is a heuristic.

4

Progressive alignment

Feng and Doolittle (1987) proposed the idea behind ClustalW (Thompson et al. 1994) and most tools since. Align the most similar sequences first, while the evidence is strongest, and add more distant ones later.

  1. Distances. Align every pair and turn identity into a distance: d = 1 − identity.
  2. Guide tree. Cluster the distances into a tree (UPGMA here; ClustalW used neighbor-joining).
  3. Merge up the tree. At each node, align two groups, sequence to sequence or profile to profile, and freeze the result.

A profile–profile alignment is Needleman–Wunsch with columns in place of residues. The score of two columns is the average BLOSUM62 score over every pair of residues, one from each group.

    Once a gap, always a gap

    Gaps are colored and labelled by the merge step that created them. A gap placed at step 1 is never reconsidered. Later steps can only add whole gap columns around it. If an early merge puts a gap in the wrong place, every later sequence has to be aligned around that mistake. This is the central weakness of progressive alignment.

    Try this

    • With the guide tree and gaps 10/1, check the stats: both heme histidines are in one column each.
    • Change gap extend to 0.5. Long gap runs get cheap, and leghemoglobin's distal histidine slides 18 columns out of place.
    • Choose "LBA, HBA, …". Starting from the most distant pair puts early, poorly supported gaps into the alignment. Compare the SP score with the guide tree's.

    Beyond progressive alignment

    Modern programs keep the progressive backbone but add ways to repair its mistakes or avoid them:

    IdeaWhat it doesUsed by
    Iterative refinementSplit the finished MSA into two groups, realign them, keep the change if the score improves. Repeat. Undoes some "once a gap" errors.MUSCLE, MAFFT (-i modes)
    ConsistencyBefore aligning, collect all pairwise alignments into a library. Reward residue pairings that agree across many pairs (if a~b and b~c, then a~c).T-Coffee, ProbCons, MAFFT L-INS-i
    Profile HMMsAlign groups as HMMs rather than as frequency profiles, and use fast embedded guide trees for huge sets.Clustal Omega
    Fast homology detectionUse a Fourier transform on residue properties to find matching segments quickly before detailed alignment.MAFFT
    Phylogeny-aware gapsTell insertions from deletions using the tree, so an insertion is not penalized again in every descendant.PRANK

    Part 2 · Using MSA tools

    7

    Running the tools

    For a few dozen sequences, the EBI multiple sequence alignment services run Clustal Omega, MAFFT, MUSCLE and T-Coffee in the browser. Paste FASTA, pick a tool, and download the result. On the command line:

    # Clustal Omega, Clustal-format output clustalo -i globins.fasta -o globins.aln --outfmt=clustal # MAFFT: automatic strategy; or L-INS-i, accurate for up to ~200 sequences mafft --auto globins.fasta > globins_mafft.fasta mafft --localpair --maxiterate 1000 globins.fasta > globins_linsi.fasta # MUSCLE version 5 muscle -align globins.fasta -output globins_muscle.fasta
    globins.fasta

    Align the input with two different programs and compare them with the result on this page. Where they agree, you can be more confident. Where they disagree, the alignment is uncertain. Open the result in Jalview to color it, edit by hand, and see conservation and consensus tracks. Common formats are aligned FASTA (gaps as -), Clustal (.aln, in blocks with the conservation line), Stockholm (.sto, used by HMMER and Pfam), and PHYLIP (used by phylogenetics programs).

    8

    Judging an alignment

    A program always returns an alignment, even for sequences that are not homologous. The score alone cannot tell you whether it is right. Useful checks:

    • Known functional residues. Do the residues that must match line up, like the heme histidines on this page? This is the fastest sanity check.
    • Structure. Benchmarks such as BAliBASE compare programs against reference alignments made from 3D structures. If structures exist for your family, use them the same way.
    • Agreement between methods. Columns that every program aligns the same way are reliable. Regions that change with the program or with the gap costs are not.
    • Gappy, variable regions. Loops and termini are often not truly alignable. For phylogenetics, trim them (trimAl, Gblocks) rather than let them add noise.
    • Input quality. Fragments, non-homologous sequences and proteins with different domain architectures distort everything. Check your inputs with BLAST or HMMER first.
    9

    Check yourself

    1. What does a single column of an MSA claim?

    That every residue in it descends from the same position in the common ancestor. A gap in the column claims that the sequence lost that position, or that the others gained it.

    2. Why can't we just extend Needleman–Wunsch to five globins?

    The matrix becomes five-dimensional: about 151⁵ ≈ 8 × 10¹⁰ cells, each checking 31 predecessors. That is roughly 2.5 × 10¹² operations for five short proteins, and the cost multiplies by about 300 with each added sequence. Maximizing the SP score is NP-hard in general.

    3. Why does progressive alignment start with the most similar sequences?

    Close relatives can be aligned with little ambiguity, so the gaps placed early are likely to be right. Because early gaps are frozen, it is best to make the most reliable decisions first and the uncertain ones last.

    4. What does "once a gap, always a gap" mean, and which methods address it?

    A gap introduced during an early merge is never removed or moved later. Iterative refinement (MUSCLE, MAFFT) realigns subgroups to fix such errors, and consistency methods (T-Coffee) try to avoid them by using information from all pairs before any merge.

    5. With gap extend 0.5, leghemoglobin's distal histidine is misaligned. Why can a cheaper gap make the alignment worse?

    Cheap long gaps let the aligner slide a whole block of the distant sequence sideways to pick up a few extra matches elsewhere. The result may score about as well, but it breaks the true column structure. Gap costs encode what we believe about how often indels happen.

    6. Two programs give different alignments of a loop region. What do you do before building a tree?

    Treat the loop as unreliable. Trim it (for example with trimAl), or run the analysis with and without it to see whether conclusions change.
