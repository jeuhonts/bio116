Bio 116 · Tutorial · Structural alignment

Superposing the Globins

Soybean leghemoglobin and human β-globin share only about one residue in five, yet they fold into the same shape. Sequence alignment struggles at that distance. Structural alignment compares the proteins' 3D coordinates directly. In this tutorial you superpose real crystal structures, measure how alike they are with RMSD and TM-score, and see where sequence and structure disagree.

Part 1 · Comparing shapes

1

Why compare structures?

Chothia and Lesk (1986) superposed pairs of related proteins and found a smooth relationship: the less identical the sequences, the more their cores differ, but slowly. Proteins in the twilight zone of sequence identity still keep the same fold. Selection acts on function, and function depends on shape, so the shape changes more slowly than the sequence that encodes it.

That makes structure the best evidence of homology when sequences have drifted far apart. It also gives a better reference alignment: residues that sit in the same place in 3D are the best guess for residues that descend from the same ancestral position. The benchmark alignments used to test MSA programs (BAliBASE, from the multiple alignment tutorial) are built this way.

2

The structures

This page uses real X-ray crystal structures from the Protein Data Bank (PDB), reduced to one point per residue: the alpha carbon (Cα). The Cα trace shows the path of the backbone, which is all a fold comparison needs. Every chain's sequence matches the UniProt sequence used in the earlier tutorials.

Lysozyme and cytochrome c are controls: real proteins with different folds. Whale myoglobin 1A6M lacks its last two residues, which are disordered in the crystal. The AlphaFold model is used in section 7.

3

Superposition and RMSD

Given pairs of equivalent residues, the best superposition is the rotation and translation of one structure that minimizes the sum of squared distances between paired Cα atoms. This has an exact solution (Kabsch 1976; Horn 1987): center both sets of points, then find the rotation from a small matrix calculation. The page computes it with Horn's quaternion method.

RMSD = √( (1/N) Σᵢ dᵢ² ) dᵢ = distance between paired Cα atoms after superposition, in Å

RMSD is intuitive: about 1 Å for close relatives, 2–3 Å for distant ones. It has two weaknesses. It depends on which pairs you include: add a few badly matched loops and RMSD shoots up even if the core is identical. And it says nothing about how many residues were matched: 1 Å over 20 residues is not better than 2 Å over 140. Always report RMSD together with the number of aligned residues.

In the viewer, switch "Superpose using" to Sequence alignment for leghemoglobin onto β-globin. The superposition then uses residue pairs from the sequence alignment, and some of those pairs are wrong, which drags the fit.

4

TM-score

The TM-score (Zhang & Skolnick 2004) fixes both problems. Every aligned pair contributes between 0 and 1 according to how close it is. Distant pairs contribute almost nothing instead of dominating. The sum is divided by the length of the reference protein, so matching more residues is rewarded.

TM-score = (1/L) Σᵢ 1 / (1 + (dᵢ/d₀)²) d₀ = 1.24 · ∛(L − 15) − 1.8 L = length of the reference protein maximized over superpositions range 0 to 1

d₀ grows with protein size, so scores can be compared between small and large proteins. As rough guides: random pairs of unrelated structures score around 0.17 or less, and pairs above about 0.5 usually share a fold (Xu & Zhang 2010). In the viewer, compare the globin pairs with lysozyme and cytochrome c.

Part 2 · Aligning by structure

5

Structure-based alignment

Superposition needs residue pairs, and good residue pairs need a superposition. Structure aligners break this circle by iterating. The version on this page follows the strategy of TM-align (Zhang & Skolnick 2005):

  1. Start from several guesses. Real TM-align uses secondary-structure matching, gapless threading (sliding one chain past the other without gaps) and a hybrid of the two. This page uses the sequence alignment and gapless threading at many offsets.
  2. For each guess, find the superposition that maximizes TM-score.
  3. Build a new score matrix, one entry for every residue pair, of 1/(1 + (d/d₀)²) using the current superposition.
  4. Run dynamic programming on that matrix, with a gap-opening penalty of 0.6 and no extension penalty, to get a new alignment.
  5. Repeat steps 2–4 until the TM-score stops improving. Keep the best result.

This is Needleman–Wunsch again. The only change is that the scores come from distances in space instead of a substitution matrix.

Where sequence and structure disagree

Computing…

For the close pairs (α–β, myoglobin–β) the two methods agree almost everywhere and give nearly the same TM-score. For leghemoglobin they part ways: roughly half of the residue pairs change, and the structure-based alignment scores much higher. The sequence alignment is not badly wrong about the fold. It is wrong about which residues are equivalent across long stretches, because at 20% identity the sequence signal is too weak to place the gaps.

The heme histidines show the subtlety. In the structure alignment, leghemoglobin's proximal histidine F8 pairs with β-globin's, with the Cα atoms under 2 Å apart. Its distal histidine E7 does not: after superposition it sits about 4.5 Å from β-globin's, and the structurally matching position in β-globin is a lysine one helical turn along. The fold is the same, but leghemoglobin's distal pocket is built differently. Structurally equivalent and functionally equivalent are not always the same residue.

7

Predicted structures

Until recently, structure comparison needed an experimental structure for both proteins. AlphaFold (Jumper et al. 2021) changed that. The AlphaFold Protein Structure Database (Varadi et al. 2022) holds predicted models for most of UniProt, each with a per-residue confidence score, pLDDT, from 0 to 100.

Choose AlphaFold vs crystal in the viewer. It superposes the AlphaFold model of soybean leghemoglobin onto the 1BIN crystal structure. The model's average pLDDT is about 96. AlphaFold classes pLDDT of 90 or more as very high confidence, and the two agree to well under 1 Å over the whole chain. For a well-studied family like the globins, predicted models are as good as experiment for fold comparison. For proteins with low pLDDT regions, treat those parts of the model with caution.

Classifications group structures by fold and evolutionary relationship. SCOP places the globins in the all-α class, "Globin-like" fold. CATH gives class 1 (mainly α), and the globin superfamily is 1.10.490.10. Looking up a protein's classification is often the fastest way to learn which distant relatives to compare it with.

9

Check yourself

1. Why does structure stay similar longer than sequence?

Function depends on shape, so selection keeps the fold while allowing many substitutions that the fold tolerates. Many different sequences can fold into the same structure.

2. Structure A superposes on B with 1.0 Å RMSD over 25 residues; structure C with 2.2 Å over 135. Which is more similar to B?

Probably C. RMSD alone ignores coverage: 25 residues could be one well-matched helix in otherwise different proteins. TM-score, which rewards the number of close pairs, would settle it.

3. What does a TM-score of 0.70 between leghemoglobin and β-globin tell you? And 0.29 for lysozyme against β-globin?

Above 0.5 means the same fold, which is strong evidence of homology here. 0.29 is well below 0.5, so lysozyme and β-globin share no fold. It is above the random level (about 0.17) because both proteins contain α-helices, and a few helices can superpose by chance.

4. How is a structure-based alignment built from coordinates?

Superpose, score every residue pair by its distance, run dynamic programming on those scores to get an alignment, and repeat until the score stops improving.

5. Leghemoglobin's distal histidine does not superpose onto β-globin's. Does that mean the alignment is wrong?

Not necessarily. The structural alignment reports which residues occupy equivalent positions in space. Leghemoglobin's distal pocket differs, so its E7 histidine sits about one turn along helix E from β-globin's. Structural and functional equivalence can differ.

6. You have a new protein with no detectable sequence homologs. How could structure help?

Predict its structure with AlphaFold (or fetch it from AlphaFold DB), then search it against the PDB and AlphaFold DB with Foldseek or DALI. A TM-score above 0.5 to a known family is a strong lead that sequence methods would miss.
