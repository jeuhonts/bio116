Bio 116 · Tutorial · Database searching

Inside BLAST

Smith–Waterman finds the best local alignment, but it is far too slow to run against every protein ever sequenced. BLAST gets close to the same answer thousands of times faster by skipping almost the whole matrix. In this tutorial you take BLAST apart one step at a time, run a working mini-BLAST on the globins from the last tutorial, and then learn to run the real thing and read its output.

  • About 50 minutes
  • Follows: Aligning the Globins
  • All tools run in your browser
  1. 1Neighborhood wordsList every 3-letter word that scores ≥ T against each query word.
  2. 2Hits on a diagonalLook up database words; act only on two nearby hits on one diagonal.
  3. 3Ungapped X-dropExtend both ways; stop when the score falls X below its best.
  4. 4Gapped extensionAlign promising segments again, now allowing gaps.
  5. 5E-valueAsk how many hits this good chance alone would produce.

By the end you should be able to

  • Explain why exhaustive dynamic programming is impractical for database search.
  • Describe the seed, extend and evaluate steps of BLAST and the parameters that control each (w, T, A, X).
  • Predict how changing T trades speed against sensitivity.
  • Convert a raw score into a bit score and an E-value, and explain why E depends on database size.
  • Choose the right BLAST program, run a search, and read a hit table and pairwise alignment critically.

Part 1 · How BLAST works

1

Why not just run Smith–Waterman?

Smith–Waterman fills one cell for every pair of residues. Searching a query of length m against a database of total length n means filling m × n cells. The tool below times the affine-gap Smith–Waterman code from the last tutorial in your own browser, then scales that up to real databases.

Smith–Waterman on your device Query: β-globin, 146 residues

Database sizes are rounded. UniProtKB/Swiss-Prot, the curated protein database, holds roughly 570,000 sequences. NCBI's nr (non-redundant) protein database holds hundreds of millions. BLAST searches Swiss-Prot in seconds.

Most of those cells are wasted. Two unrelated proteins produce a matrix full of zeros and small numbers. The only interesting part of the matrix is a narrow band near a few diagonals. BLAST (Altschul et al. 1990; 1997) finds those diagonals cheaply first and runs expensive alignment only there. It gives up the guarantee of finding the optimal alignment. In return it gets speed, and in practice it misses little that Smith–Waterman would have scored as significant.

2

Step 1 · Words and the neighborhood

BLAST breaks the query into overlapping words of length w. For proteins w = 3. Requiring exact word matches would miss conservative substitutions, so BLAST also accepts any word that scores at least T against the query word with BLOSUM62. That set is the word's neighborhood. The default is T = 11.

score(HGK, HGR) = s(H,H) + s(G,G) + s(K,R) = 8 + 6 + 2 = 16 ≥ 11 → in the neighborhood score(HGK, AGK) = s(H,A) + s(G,G) + s(K,K) = −2 + 6 + 5 = 9 < 11 → not
Neighborhood explorer Click any residue in β-globin to pick the word starting there

Notice two things. Words made of rare, high-scoring residues (W, C, H) have large neighborhoods, because they have points to spare. Words made of common, low-scoring residues (A, S, L) may not even reach T against themselves, so they contribute no seeds. Lowering T makes BLAST more sensitive but multiplies the number of words to look up.

3

Step 2 · Finding hits in the database

All the neighborhood words for the whole query go into a lookup table that maps each word to the query positions it came from. BLAST then walks along each database sequence once, reading every 3-letter word and looking it up. Each match is a hit at query position i and subject position j. Hits that belong to the same ungapped alignment share a diagonal, j − i.

Extending every hit would still be slow, and most hits are noise. The 1997 version of BLAST uses the two-hit method. It only extends when it finds two non-overlapping hits on the same diagonal within A = 40 residues of each other. A real similarity usually produces several nearby hits. A random one rarely does.

4

Step 3 · Ungapped extension with X-drop

From a triggering hit, BLAST extends the alignment along the diagonal without gaps, first to the right and then to the left. It keeps a running score and remembers the best score seen so far. As soon as the running score falls more than X below that best, it stops, then trims back to the best point. The result is a high-scoring segment pair (HSP).

X is set in bits and converted to raw score. The default of 7 bits is about 15 BLOSUM62 points. A small X stops quickly and can cut a real alignment short at a bad patch. A large X explores further but spends more time on noise.

5

Step 4 · Gapped extension

Real homologs have indels, so an ungapped HSP is usually only a piece of the true alignment. If an HSP scores above a gap trigger (22 bits, about 42 raw points with BLOSUM62), BLAST runs a gapped dynamic-programming alignment outward from a point inside it. This is Smith–Waterman limited by its own X-drop rule, so it explores only the cells near the HSP. The default gap costs for blastp are 11 to open a gap and 1 per residue.

BLAST gap cost for a gap of length L = existence + L × extension = 11 + L (In last tutorial's notation this is open = 12, extend = 1.)

The mini-BLAST below uses full Smith–Waterman on the query–subject pair for this step. For sequences this short, that gives the same or a slightly better alignment than BLAST's X-drop version.

6

Step 5 · Scores, bits and E-values

In the last tutorial you estimated significance by shuffling. Karlin and Altschul (1990) worked out the answer directly. For local alignments of random sequences, the number of distinct alignments scoring at least S by chance is expected to be:

E = K · m · n · e^(−λS) bit score S′ = (λS − ln K) / ln 2 so E = m · n · 2^(−S′) P-value P = 1 − e^(−E) (P ≈ E when E is small) m = query length n = database length (all residues) λ, K depend on matrix and gap costs BLOSUM62, gaps 11/1: λ = 0.267 K = 0.041

The raw score depends on the scoring system. The bit score rescales it by λ and K, so bit scores from different matrices are comparable. The E-value adds the size of the search. Every extra doubling of the database doubles E. The same alignment can therefore be convincing against a small database and unremarkable against a huge one.

E-value calculator Starts at β-globin vs α-globin

Try S = 108, the score of whale myoglobin against α-globin. It is overwhelming against the mini database, still significant against Swiss-Prot, and weak against nr. Real BLAST also corrects m and n for edge effects and adjusts for amino-acid composition, so its numbers differ slightly.

    One step further: PSI-BLAST

    A single query sequence tells BLAST only which residue sits at each position of one protein. The family tells you more: which positions never change (the F8 histidine), which accept any hydrophobic residue, and which vary freely. PSI-BLAST (Position-Specific Iterated BLAST; Altschul et al. 1997) learns this from its own hits and uses it to search again.

    1. Run an ordinary BLAST search.
    2. Take every hit with E below the inclusion threshold (0.005 by default) and stack its alignment under the query, column by column.
    3. From the residues in each column, build a position-specific scoring matrix (PSSM): one row of 20 scores for every query position.
    4. Search the database again, scoring with the PSSM instead of BLOSUM62.
    5. Repeat until no new sequence passes the threshold. The search has converged.
    f(i,a) = fraction of the sequences in column i that have residue a g(i,a) = Σ_b f(i,b) · q(b,a) / p(b) pseudocounts from BLOSUM62's target frequencies Q(i,a) = (α·f(i,a) + β·g(i,a)) / (α + β) α = sequences in column − 1, β = 10 score(i,a) = round( 2 · log₂( Q(i,a) / p(a) ) ) half-bits, like BLOSUM62 With only the query in a column (α = 0), score(i,a) is just the BLOSUM62 row for that residue.

    The pseudocounts matter. With four sequences in a column you have seen very few of the residues that could appear there. g(i,a) spreads some probability to residues that BLOSUM62 says are similar to the ones observed. As more sequences join, α grows and the observed frequencies take over.

    Inside the PSSM

    What happened

    Start from β-globin with the defaults. Iteration 1 is plain BLAST: it finds α-globin and misses both myoglobins, as in the mini-BLAST above. The profile built from α and β is enough to pull in human and whale myoglobin in iteration 2, and the search converges at iteration 3. Look at the F8 region of the PSSM. In iteration 1 the proximal histidine row is just BLOSUM62's H row: +8 for histidine, +2 for tyrosine. Once all four animal globins agree on histidine, it rises to +9 and the scores for the other residues fall.

    Leghemoglobin never gets in. Its "aligned anyway" E-value barely moves in iteration 2, then jumps to about 0.25 once the myoglobins are in the profile. The profile is getting better at recognizing animal globins and worse at everything else. PSI-BLAST reaches distant relatives by stepping through intermediates. Each round admits sequences a little further from the query, and those widen the profile for the next round. A real database has thousands of globins from vertebrates, invertebrates, plants and bacteria to act as stepping stones. This nine-sequence database has none between the animal globins and leghemoglobin, so there is no path across.

    Try it for real. On the NCBI Protein BLAST page, set the database to UniProtKB/Swiss-Prot and choose PSI-BLAST under Program Selection. Search with β-globin (P68871), then press "Run PSI-Blast iteration 2" and so on. Note at which iteration non-vertebrate globins first appear, and whether leghemoglobin ever does.

    Profile corruption. The same feedback loop can go wrong. If one unrelated sequence slips under the threshold, it enters the profile and pulls in its own relatives, and later iterations drift away from your family. Check what joins at each round. NCBI lets you untick hits so they stay out of the next profile. Profile HMM tools such as jackhmmer (HMMER) iterate in the same way and carry the same risk.

    Choosing a program

    The program depends on what your query is and what you want to search. When the question is about proteins, search at the protein level whenever you can. The genetic code's redundancy makes DNA diverge faster than the protein it encodes, so protein comparisons see further back in time.

    ProgramQueryDatabaseTypical use
    blastpproteinproteinFind homologs of a protein. What we did above.
    blastnnucleotidenucleotideFind where a DNA sequence comes from; primers, repeats. megablast (word size 28) for near-identical matches, blastn task (word size 11) for more distant ones.
    blastxnucleotide, translated in 6 framesproteinWhat protein might this DNA or RNA read encode?
    tblastnproteinnucleotide, translated in 6 framesFind unannotated genes for a protein in a genome or transcript set.
    tblastxnucleotide, translatednucleotide, translatedCompare two DNA sets at the protein level. Very slow; use sparingly.

    Running real BLAST

    On the NCBI website

    1. Go to blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov and choose Protein BLAST.
    2. Paste your query as plain sequence or FASTA, or enter an accession such as P68871.
    3. Pick a database. UniProtKB/Swiss-Prot (swissprot) is small and well annotated. nr is comprehensive but full of unreviewed and duplicate entries.
    4. Optionally limit the search to an organism or exclude one, such as Homo sapiens, to see only other species.
    5. Under Algorithm parameters you can find the expect threshold, word size, matrix (BLOSUM62 by default) and gap costs (11/1 by default). Leave them alone for a first search.
    6. Press BLAST. Results come back as Descriptions (the hit table), Graphic Summary, Alignments and Taxonomy tabs.

    On the command line with BLAST+

    For many queries, a custom database, or reproducible analysis, install BLAST+ (for example conda install -c bioconda blast). Save the mini database below as globins.fasta and β-globin as hbb.fasta, then:

    # 1. Build a searchable database from a FASTA file makeblastdb -in globins.fasta -dbtype prot -out globins # 2. Search it, keeping hits with E ≤ 0.001, as a tab-separated table blastp -query hbb.fasta -db globins -evalue 1e-3 -outfmt 6 # 3. Or search NCBI's copy of Swiss-Prot over the internet blastp -query hbb.fasta -db swissprot -remote -evalue 1e-3 -outfmt 6 -max_target_seqs 50

    ExpectThe E-value: chance hits this good expected in this search. Your main filter.
    IdentitiesIdentical columns over alignment length. Only meaningful alongside that length.

    Rules of thumb

    What you seeReasonable reading
    E ≤ 10⁻³ in a protein searchVery likely homologous (Pearson 2013). Tiny E-values such as 10⁻⁵⁰ mean close relatives, not "more homologous".
    10⁻³ < E < 1Gray zone. Check coverage, conserved functional residues, and a reverse search with the hit as query.
    E ≥ 1Expected by chance. BLAST can still miss real homologs (see leghemoglobin).
    High E-value, high identity, short alignmentA short stretch such as a low-complexity or repeat region. Identity alone means little over 20 residues.
    Low E-value but low query coverageA shared domain, not a whole-protein relationship. Do not transfer whole-protein function.
    Top hit labeled "hypothetical" or "predicted"Annotation is usually inferred from an earlier BLAST. Look further down for an experimentally characterized hit (Swiss-Prot helps here).

    Homology is not function. α- and β-globin are clear homologs with similar jobs. But gene duplication lets paralogs change function, so a significant hit tells you about shared ancestry first and shared function only with further evidence. To find a likely ortholog in another species, use a reciprocal best hit. Search species B with your protein, take the top hit, and search it back against species A. If it returns your original protein, the two are good ortholog candidates.

    When BLAST comes up empty. No hit is not proof that there are no homologs. For distant relatives, try PSI-BLAST, profile HMM tools such as HMMER, or structure comparison.

    Check yourself

    1. Why does protein BLAST seed with neighborhood words instead of exact 3-letter matches?

    Related proteins often differ by conservative substitutions, so exact word matches would miss many real seeds. Scoring words with BLOSUM62 and accepting everything ≥ T lets a word like HGR seed from HGK, while keeping the list short enough to look up quickly.

    2. You raise T from 11 to 13. What happens to speed and to sensitivity?

    Neighborhoods shrink, so there are fewer words to look up, fewer hits and fewer extensions. The search gets faster. Some real but weak similarities lose their seeds and are missed, so sensitivity drops.

    3. The same alignment has E = 2 × 10⁻⁹ against the mini database and about 4 × 10⁻⁴ against Swiss-Prot. Which is right?

    Both. E is the number of chance hits expected in that search, and it grows in proportion to database size. Searching more sequences gives chance more opportunities. Report the E-value for the database you actually searched.

    4. Why compare hits by bit score rather than raw score?

    Raw scores depend on the matrix and gap costs. Bit scores are rescaled by λ and K, so a 50-bit hit means the same thing whatever scoring system produced it. The bit score also does not change with database size, unlike E.

    5. A hit has E = 10⁻⁴⁰ but covers only residues 300–420 of your 900-residue query. How do you interpret it?

    The two proteins share one domain with near certainty. That says nothing about the other 780 residues. Annotate the domain, not the whole protein.

    6. BLAST with β-globin never finds soybean leghemoglobin, yet they are homologous. Why, and what could you try?

    At about 20% identity, their best ungapped segment scores below the gap trigger, and the gapped alignment would not be significant anyway. The sequences sit in the twilight zone. Profile methods (PSI-BLAST, HMMER) pool information from many family members and can detect such relationships, provided the database has intermediate sequences to bridge the gap. Structure comparison can too.

    8. In the mini database, PSI-BLAST from β-globin finds the myoglobins in iteration 2 but never finds leghemoglobin. Why does iteration help with one and not the other?

    Myoglobin is about 25% identical to α- and β-globin. A profile built from α and β captures the positions all animal globins share, so myoglobin scores higher against the profile than against β alone. Leghemoglobin is further from every sequence in the profile, and there is no intermediate sequence that could enter first and widen the profile toward it. Each iteration can only reach sequences within range of what the profile already contains.

    9. What is profile corruption, and how do you guard against it?

    An unrelated sequence passes the inclusion threshold, enters the PSSM, and biases later iterations toward its own family. Guard against it by checking new members at each iteration, excluding doubtful ones, keeping a strict inclusion threshold, and confirming key hits with an independent method.

    7. Why does blastp usually beat blastn for finding homologs of a protein-coding gene?

    Synonymous changes let DNA diverge while the protein stays the same. Protein alphabets and substitution matrices also carry more information per position than 4-letter DNA with simple match/mismatch scores. So protein comparisons detect relationships over longer evolutionary distances.
