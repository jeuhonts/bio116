Aligning the Globins

Hemoglobin α, hemoglobin β, myoglobin and soybean leghemoglobin all fold into the same eight-helix shape and hold heme between the same two histidines. Their sequences have drifted much further apart than their structures have. In this tutorial you score alignments of these proteins, run the Needleman–Wunsch and Smith–Waterman algorithms one cell at a time, and test whether a weak match could be chance.

  • About 45 minutes
  • Assumes: amino-acid chemistry, basic probability
  • All tools run in your browser

By the end you should be able to

  • Read an alignment: identities, conservative substitutions and gaps.
  • Explain where BLOSUM62 scores come from and read the matrix.
  • Fill a dynamic-programming matrix for global and local alignment, then trace it back.
  • Choose gap penalties and predict their effect on real globins.
  • Judge an alignment score against scores from shuffled sequences.
1

Meet the globins

Globins were the first proteins to have their 3D structures solved. Kendrew solved sperm whale myoglobin in 1958 and Perutz solved hemoglobin soon after. Every globin folds into helices named A through H. The heme iron is bound by the proximal histidine F8, the 8th residue of helix F. On the other side of the heme, where O2 binds, sits the distal histidine E7.

Those two histidines give us a check that has nothing to do with the alignment algorithm. If an alignment of two globins is right, their E7 histidines should land in the same column, and so should their F8 histidines.

2

What an alignment says

An alignment is a hypothesis about which residues descend from the same position in an ancestral protein. Each column is one of three kinds:

  • Identity (|): the same residue in both. The position may be constrained, or there may not have been time for it to change.
  • Substitution: different residues. A similar pair such as L/V or K/R gets : and a dissimilar pair gets .
  • Gap (-): one sequence gained or lost residues since the two split. This is an indel (insertion or deletion), and the alignment cannot tell you which one happened.

Homology is yes or no. Two proteins either share an ancestor or they do not. Percent identity and alignment scores are measurements, and they are the evidence for homology. "These proteins are 43% homologous" is a common mistake. Say "43% identical", or "homologous, with 43% identity".

3

Scoring substitutions: BLOSUM62

Some substitutions happen all the time in real proteins. Isoleucine and valine are both small and hydrophobic, so one can often replace the other in a helix core. Other swaps, like tryptophan to glycine, almost never survive selection. A good scoring scheme should reward the first kind and penalize the second.

Henikoff and Henikoff (1992) counted aligned residue pairs in conserved, gap-free blocks of related proteins. Sequences more than 62% identical were clustered together so that close relatives would not dominate the counts. For each pair they compared the observed frequency qab with the frequency expected by chance, papb:

s(a,b) = round( 2 · log₂( q_ab / (p_a · p_b) ) ) units: half-bits

A positive score means the pair turns up in real alignments more often than chance predicts. A negative score means less often. Because these are logarithms, the scores of independent columns can simply be added. An alignment score is a log-likelihood ratio: "related" against "unrelated".

Look along the diagonal. W–W scores +11 but A–A only +4. Tryptophan is rare, so two tryptophans lining up by chance is unlikely, and a W match tells you more.

4

Paying for gaps

Without a gap penalty, an aligner could scatter gaps everywhere to line up a few extra identities. A linear penalty charges g for every gap position. An affine penalty charges more to open a gap than to extend it:

linear: cost(L) = g · L affine: cost(L) = open + (L − 1) · extend

Affine penalties match how proteins change. A single mutation event often inserts or deletes several residues together: a loop grows, or a helix is lost. Hemoglobin α has no D helix at all, and that shows up as one run of gap positions when α is aligned to β.

Gap patternLinear, g = 8Affine, 10 / 0.5
One gap of 5 residues4012
Five gaps of 1 residue4050

Under the linear scheme both patterns cost the same. The affine scheme strongly prefers the single block. Open 10 / extend 0.5 are the defaults of EMBOSS Needle, and this page uses them for full-length alignments.

5

Needleman–Wunsch, one cell at a time

You cannot try every alignment. Two 150-residue globins can be aligned in roughly 1089 ways. Needleman and Wunsch (1970) noticed that the best alignment of two prefixes can be built from best alignments of slightly shorter prefixes. Cell F(i, j) holds the best score for aligning the first i residues of A with the first j residues of B. For 150 × 150 residues that is 22,500 cells.

F(0,0) = 0 F(i,0) = −i·g F(0,j) = −j·g F(i,j) = max { F(i−1, j−1) + s(aᵢ, bⱼ) ↖ pair aᵢ with bⱼ F(i−1, j) − g ↑ aᵢ against a gap F(i, j−1) − g ← bⱼ against a gap }

Once the matrix is full, the score of the best global alignment is in the bottom-right cell. Traceback follows the arrows from that cell back to the top-left corner to recover the alignment. To keep a single matrix on screen, this explorer uses a linear gap penalty.

6

Smith–Waterman: local alignment

Global alignment forces every residue of both sequences into the answer. That is wrong when only part of two proteins is related: a shared domain, a motif inside a longer protein, or a short query searched against a database. Smith and Waterman (1981) made three changes:

F(i,j) = max { 0, F(i−1,j−1) + s(aᵢ,bⱼ), F(i−1,j) − g, F(i,j−1) − g } 1. Add 0 as a fourth choice: a bad stretch resets to zero instead of dragging the score down. 2. Start traceback from the highest cell anywhere in the matrix. 3. Stop traceback when you reach a cell holding 0.

Switch the explorer above to Local (SW). Row 0 and column 0 become zeros, negative numbers disappear, and the traceback path can start and end in the middle of the matrix. Local alignment is also the core of database search, which the BLAST tutorial builds on.

How far does the family reach?

Here is every pair of the five globins aligned globally with BLOSUM62 and gap 10 / 0.5. Each cell is percent identity: identical columns divided by alignment length.

Above about 40% identity over 100+ residues, homology is nearly certain. Below about 25% you are in the twilight zone (Doolittle 1986; Rost 1999). Unrelated proteins reach 20% identity by chance often enough that identity alone cannot settle the question. Leghemoglobin and the animal globins share a fold and a proximal histidine, so they are homologous. Their sequences, though, sit right at the edge of what alignment can detect. The next section shows one way to put a number on that.

9

Is the score significant?

A score only means something next to the scores that unrelated sequences get. One simple test keeps sequence A, shuffles sequence B many times, and aligns A to each shuffle. Shuffling keeps B's length and amino-acid composition but destroys its order, so any evolutionary signal is gone. If the real score sits far to the right of the shuffled scores, chance is a poor explanation.

z = (S_real − mean_shuffled) / sd_shuffled empirical p = (number of shuffles scoring ≥ S_real + 1) / (N + 1)
Shuffle test Local alignment, BLOSUM62, gap 10 / 0.5

Treat the z-score as a rough guide. The best local score from random sequences does not follow a normal distribution. It follows an extreme-value (Gumbel) distribution, whose right tail is longer than a normal curve's. So a z of 3 is less impressive than a normal table suggests. BLAST fits this extreme-value distribution directly and reports an E-value: the number of hits this good you would expect by chance. That is the subject of the next tutorial.

10

Check yourself

1. In a global alignment with linear gap penalty g, what goes in cell F(i, 0)?

−i·g. The only way to align the first i residues of A with nothing from B is to put all i residues against gaps.

2. Why does a local alignment matrix never contain a negative number?

Zero is one of the choices in the max. If every way of extending an alignment into a cell scores below zero, it is better to start a new alignment there. That reset lets a strong local match stand out from unrelated flanking sequence.

3. BLOSUM62 scores C–C as +9 but L–L as +4. Give two reasons a cysteine match carries more information.

Cysteine is rare (low pC), so chance C–C pairs are unlikely. Cysteines are also often held in place by disulfide bonds or metal binding, so they are strongly conserved in real alignments (high qCC). Both push the log-odds ratio up.

4. With gap open 1, α vs β gets more than 20 gaps and almost the same identity as with gap open 10. Why is it the worse alignment?

With cheap gaps the aligner breaks the sequences into short pieces to squeeze out small score gains. Many of those gaps are not real indels. They are fitting noise. The structures of α and β superimpose helix by helix, with one real block difference (the D helix). An alignment full of one-residue gaps contradicts that structural evidence.

5. β-globin and soybean leghemoglobin are about 20% identical. Is that enough to call them homologous?

Not from identity alone, because unrelated sequences can reach 20%. The shuffle test adds evidence: the real score sits far above every shuffled score, around z = 7. Independent evidence settles it. The two proteins share the globin fold and bind heme through an F-helix proximal histidine that aligns in the same column.

6. When would you choose local over global alignment for two proteins?

When you expect only part of them to be related: a shared domain in otherwise different proteins, a short motif, a fragment against a full-length protein, or a query against a database. Use global when you expect the two to be related along their whole length, as with α and β globin.
