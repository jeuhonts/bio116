Stage 1 of 5

Immunoprecipitation

Enriching the DNA your protein was sitting on

Cells are treated with formaldehyde, which crosslinks proteins to the DNA they touch. Chromatin is sheared to 200–500 bp, and an antibody against the protein of interest pulls down its fragments. Crosslinks are reversed, and the recovered DNA is made into a library as in Part 1.

The pull-down is never pure: most reads still come from random background DNA. A matched input control (sheared chromatin with no antibody) shows how background varies along the genome. The share of reads that fall in peaks (FRiP) summarises enrichment; ENCODE flags transcription factor experiments below about 1%.

Stage 2 of 5

From reads to signal

Why ChIP reads come in two offset piles

A bound fragment is about 200 bp, but a single-end read covers only its first 50 bp. Reads from the top strand start at the fragment's left end; reads from the bottom strand start at its right end. So a binding site shows a pile of + strand reads just upstream and a pile of − strand reads just downstream, about one fragment length apart.

Peak callers estimate the fragment length from this offset, often by strand cross-correlation, then shift each read (or extend it) toward the fragment centre. The combined signal then peaks right over the binding site.

Stage 3 of 5

Peak calling

Is this pile of reads more than background?

A peak caller such as MACS2/MACS3 slides a window along the genome and asks how surprising the ChIP read count is, given the expected background. MACS models background as Poisson and takes the local rate λ as the largest of the genome-wide average and the input's rate in 1 kb and 10 kb windows around the site. Using the input corrects for regions that give extra reads in any library: open chromatin that shears easily, copy-number gains, and repeats.

Some regions produce huge artefactual signal in almost every experiment, such as satellite repeats and rDNA. ENCODE maintains a blacklist of these, and peaks there are removed.

Stage 4 of 5

Histone marks and replicates

Not every signal is a sharp peak

Transcription factors give narrow peaks a few hundred bp wide. Histone modifications cover nucleosomes over larger stretches, and each mark has a typical shape and meaning. Sharp marks are called in narrow mode; broad marks need broad-domain callers (MACS2 --broad, SICER, epic2). Combining several marks gives chromatin states (ChromHMM).

Peaks must also reproduce. ENCODE's IDR (irreproducible discovery rate) method ranks peaks by strength in each of two replicates. Real peaks rank consistently in both; noise peaks rank randomly. Keeping peaks until the ranks stop agreeing gives a reproducible set.

Stage 5 of 5

Motif discovery

The sequence the protein recognises

Sequence-specific transcription factors bind short DNA patterns. Tools such as MEME and HOMER find words over-represented at peak summits and summarise them as a position weight matrix (PWM). A sequence logo draws it: stack height at each position is the information content in bits (maximum 2 for DNA), and letter heights are proportional to their frequency.

A PWM can then scan any sequence, adding up log-odds scores to predict new binding sites. Most predicted sites are not bound in a given cell, because chromatin accessibility and partner proteins matter too.

Wrap-up

ChIP-seq and its successors

Same question, less material, less background

ChIP-seq

  • Millions of cells; crosslinking and sonication
  • High background, so an input control is essential
  • 20–40M reads per sample
  • The reference method: ENCODE, Roadmap Epigenomics
  • Works for most proteins with a good antibody

CUT&RUN and CUT&Tag

  • Hundreds to thousands of cells, native chromatin
  • Antibody-tethered MNase or Tn5 cuts only near the target
  • Very low background; 3–10M reads suffice
  • CUT&Tag works in single cells
  • Weaker for some transcription factors and very transient binding

Modern frontiers

  • Accessibility without antibodies. ATAC-seq uses Tn5 to mark open chromatin, and footprinting within ATAC peaks infers which factors are bound.
  • Single-cell chromatin. scATAC-seq and single-cell CUT&Tag profile regulatory landscapes cell by cell, alongside RNA in multiome assays (Part 4).
  • Base-resolution binding. ChIP-exo and ChIP-nexus trim fragments with exonuclease to pinpoint contact sites.
  • Learning the grammar. Deep learning models (BPNet, Enformer, AlphaGenome) predict binding profiles and expression from DNA sequence alone, and score how variants disrupt motifs.

Simulations use simplified models for teaching: coverage is Poisson around smooth expected profiles, the peak caller is a single-window Poisson test in the style of MACS, and the replicate model uses log-normal noise. Motif sequences are generated around a CTCF-like consensus.
