BIO 116 · Bioinformatics lab 2

Finding a gene in 10,309 bases of human DNA

In lab 1 you sequenced and assembled the HRAS region. Now you have 10,309 bases and no annotation. Where is the gene? A bacterial gene is usually one long open reading frame. A human gene is broken into short exons, separated by introns that can be many times longer. Work through the six sections to find HRAS the way a gene-prediction program does: look for signals, score coding potential, put the exons together, and then check the result against RNA evidence.

The answer key, hidden from the programs

HRAS spans 3,335 bp of this entry, but only 570 bp (17%) code for protein. Those 570 bp are split across four exons.

    Section 1 · The prokaryote approach

    Open reading frames: why "find the longest ORF" fails

    An open reading frame (ORF) is a stretch that starts with ATG and runs, codon by codon, to the first stop codon (TAA, TAG or TGA). DNA can be read in six frames: three offsets on each strand.

    In random sequence, 3 of the 64 codons are stops. So a stop turns up about every 21 codons, and an ORF of 100+ codons is rare by chance. In bacteria, where ~88% of the genome is coding and genes have no introns, "long ORF = gene" works well.

    Try it on the HRAS entry. The chart marks every stop codon in all six frames and highlights ATG-initiated ORFs above your length cut-off. HRAS coding exons are drawn in the HRAS track below the frames.

    Section 2 · Signal sensors

    Splice sites: GT…AG and weight matrices

    The spliceosome recognises short sequence signals at intron boundaries. Almost every human intron starts with GT (the 5′ or donor site) and ends with AG (the 3′ or acceptor site). A branch-point A and a pyrimidine-rich tract sit just upstream of the AG.

    Beyond the GT, the flanking bases are only preferences. A position weight matrix (PWM) scores a candidate site by adding up, position by position, how much more often each base appears at real sites than by chance:

    score = Σ log₂( fpos,base / 0.25 ) bits

    The sequence logos on the right show how much each position tells you. Tall stacks carry more information.

    Section 3 · Content sensors

    Coding potential: codons are not used at random

    Signals are short and noisy. Content sensors use the statistics of long stretches instead. Protein-coding DNA is read in triplets, and organisms prefer some codons for the same amino acid. Human genes use CTG for leucine 40% of the time and CTA only 7%.

    Score each codon by how much more common it is in human genes than in this stretch of DNA overall:

    LLR(codon) = log₂( Phuman genes / Pthis entry )

    Then slide a window along the sequence and average the codon scores in each of the three frames. In an exon, the frame that is actually translated should stand out. The same idea, with longer words and Markov chains, drives GeneMark and GENSCAN.

    Frames are named by position: frame 1 reads codons starting at 1, 4, 7 … of the entry, frame 2 at 2, 5, 8 …, frame 3 at 3, 6, 9 ….

    Section 4 · Gene structure

    Build the gene model yourself

    A gene finder generates candidate exons: stretches bounded by good signals at both ends. There are four kinds:

    • initial: ATG … GT
    • internal: AG … GT
    • terminal: AG … stop codon
    • single: ATG … stop, with no introns

    Then it has to choose a chain of non-overlapping exons that stays in frame across every splice junction. An intron can fall between codons (phase 0), after the first base of a codon (phase 1) or after the second (phase 2). Leftover bases at the end of one exon join the start of the next exon to make a codon.

    Thirteen candidates from the coding window are drawn on the right. Pick exons to assemble a gene. The panel translates your model and compares it with the HRAS protein in UniProt (P01112, 189 amino acids).

    Section 5 · Ab initio prediction

    Let the algorithm choose: GENSCAN-style parsing

    Programs like GENSCAN and AUGUSTUS describe a gene as a path through a hidden Markov model. Each state is intergenic, exon or intron, and the three intron states keep track of phase. The Viterbi algorithm (dynamic programming) finds the highest-scoring path, meaning the best gene structure, out of the huge number of possible exon combinations.

    Our parser does the same search over candidate exons. Each exon's score is:

    wsignal·(splice-site bits) + wcontent·(codon LLR) + exon penalty

    Change the weights and watch the predicted structure change. Accuracy is measured two ways. Sensitivity (Sn) is the fraction of real coding bases or exons that were found. Specificity (Sp) is the fraction of predicted coding bases or exons that are real. Gene finders call it specificity, but statisticians would call it precision.

    Sn = TP / (TP + FN) Sp = TP / (TP + FP)

    Section 6 · Extrinsic evidence

    RNA-seq: letting the cell show you the exons

    Ab initio predictors only see the genomic sequence. Modern annotation pipelines (Ensembl, RefSeq, BRAKER, MAKER) also use evidence:

    • RNA-seq reads: a read that spans a splice junction maps in two pieces and marks the exact intron coordinates.
    • Protein homology: similar proteins from other species.
    • Conservation: exons change more slowly than introns.

    Here, RNA-seq reads are simulated from the real HRAS transcript (NM_005343), with some intronic reads from unspliced pre-mRNA added. Arcs join the ends of spliced reads, and the number on each arc is how many reads cross that junction. Compare the evidence with the ab initio prediction from Section 5 (weights 1 and 1, exon penalty −16, min site score 3 bits).

    Key terms

    ORF
    A run of codons from ATG to the first in-frame stop.
    CDS
    Coding sequence: the exon bases translated into protein, from start codon to stop codon.
    UTR
    Untranslated region: exon sequence before the start codon (5′) or after the stop (3′). It is in the mRNA but not the protein.
    Donor / acceptor
    The 5′ (GT) and 3′ (AG) ends of an intron.
    PWM
    Position weight matrix: base preferences at each position of a signal, used to score candidate sites.
    Intron phase
    Where an intron interrupts the reading frame: 0 between codons, 1 or 2 inside a codon.
    Content sensor
    A statistic, such as codon usage or hexamer frequency, that separates coding from non-coding DNA.
    Viterbi algorithm
    Dynamic programming that finds the single most probable path of hidden states, here the most likely gene structure.
    Sn / Sp
    Sensitivity TP/(TP+FN) and specificity TP/(TP+FP), measured per base or per exact exon.
    Spliced alignment
    Aligning an RNA read or protein to the genome while allowing large gaps at introns.

    About the data

    • All sequence is the 10,309-bp human HRAS RefSeqGene entry (NG_007666.1) in this course's triplets.txt. It has the same numbering as the other BIO 116 labs. The answer key is the RefSeq annotation: transcript NM_005343.4, protein NP_005334.1 (UniProt P01112).
    • Codon frequencies are the human codon-usage table from the Kazusa Codon Usage Database. Splice-site matrices use rounded, approximate frequencies from published human splice-site compilations. They are good enough to teach with, not to publish with.
    • The parser in Section 5 is a teaching simplification. It handles one gene on the forward strand, has no promoter, UTR or length models, and uses bit-scores, not trained HMM probabilities. GENSCAN and AUGUSTUS model all of these.
    • Section 6 reads are simulated without errors from the RefSeq exon coordinates. About 1 in 10 reads is drawn from unspliced pre-mRNA.
