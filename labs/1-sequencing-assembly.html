BIO 116 · Bioinformatics lab 1

From DNA molecules to a finished genome

No sequencer reads a chromosome end to end. We shatter DNA into millions of pieces, read each piece, then rebuild the genome from the overlaps, the way you'd rebuild a shredded book. Work through the six sections in order. Each one uses the real human HRAS sequence that runs through all four labs.

    Section 1 · First generation

    Sanger sequencing: stopping DNA synthesis on purpose

    DNA polymerase copies a template starting from a primer. Sanger's trick (1977) was to add a small amount of dideoxynucleotides (ddNTPs). A ddNTP has no 3′-OH, so once polymerase adds one, the chain can't grow any further.

    Each copy stops at a random position, so the tube ends up holding fragments of every length. Each fragment ends in a ddNTP carrying a coloured dye. Capillary electrophoresis sorts the fragments by size, shortest first, and a laser reads the colour of each fragment's last base.

    Sanger reads are ~800 bp long and ~99.99% accurate. They are still the clinical gold standard for checking a single gene, like looking for the HRAS G12V mutation in a tumour.

    Section 2 · Short-read, massively parallel

    Illumina: sequencing by synthesis on a flow cell

    The genome is sheared into ~300–500 bp fragments and adapters are ligated on (a library). Each fragment is copied in place on a glass flow cell into a cluster of ~1,000 identical molecules, so its signal is bright enough to photograph.

    Each cycle, the sequencer adds all four nucleotides, each with its own dye and a reversible terminator. (Newer instruments tell the four bases apart with just two dyes; this simulation shows the classic four.) Every strand adds exactly one base. A camera images the tile, then the dye and the block are cleaved off. One cycle = one base, for billions of clusters at once.

    Over time some molecules in a cluster fall a cycle behind (phasing) or jump ahead. The colour gets muddier and the base caller trusts it less. That confidence is the Phred quality score:

    Q = −10 · log₁₀(Perror) Q20 = 1 in 100 · Q30 = 1 in 1,000

    Section 3 · Shotgun sequencing

    Coverage: how many reads is enough?

    Reads start at random positions, so some bases get read many times and some never. Coverage (depth) is the average number of reads covering each base:

    C = N · L / G (N reads, L read length, G genome size)

    Random placement follows a Poisson distribution. The fraction of the genome left unread is about e−C (Lander & Waterman, 1988). At 1× you miss 37% of bases, and at 5× under 1%. Clinical genomes aim for 30×, so every base is read many times and real variants stand out from sequencing errors.

    Try it on the 10,309-bp HRAS entry. Find the fewest reads that leave no gaps, then change the read length and see what happens. (At 50 bp you may not reach zero gaps even with 800 reads. Why?)

    Section 4 · Assembly, method 1

    Overlap–layout–consensus: solve the jigsaw

    Without a reference genome we have to assemble de novo. The classic approach, used for the first human genome drafts, has three steps:

    1. Overlap: compare every read with every other and find where the end (suffix) of one matches the start (prefix) of another.
    2. Layout: chain the reads by their overlaps into an ordered path.
    3. Consensus: where reads stack, take the majority base. This outvotes random sequencing errors.

    The result is a contig, a continuous stretch of assembled sequence. Build one from these seven shuffled reads. Click a read to add it to the right-hand end of your contig. The assembler accepts it only if the reads overlap by at least 5 bases.

    Comparing all pairs grows as N², which is fine for 7 reads and painful for 800 million. That cost is why short-read assemblers moved to the graph in Section 5.

    Section 5 · Assembly, method 2

    De Bruijn graphs: assembling from k-mers

    Short-read assemblers (Velvet, SPAdes, MEGAHIT) skip all-against-all comparison. They chop every read into overlapping words of length k (k-mers) and build a graph:

    • each node is a (k−1)-mer,
    • each k-mer is an edge from its prefix to its suffix.

    A walk that uses every edge exactly once (an Eulerian path) spells out the genome. It can be found in linear time. OLC's layout instead needs a path that visits every read once (a Hamiltonian path), which is computationally hard.

    The catch is repeats. If a (k−1)-mer occurs twice in the genome, the two copies merge into one node and the graph gets a fork or a loop. Often several different walks then use every edge, and the k-mers alone can't tell you which one is the real genome. Try a small k on the HRAS preset, where codons 7–9 are GTG GTG GTG.

    Section 6 · Finishing

    Repeats, long reads and N50

    About half the human genome is repeats. There are ~1.2 million Alu elements (~300 bp each). The HRAS entry has two of them back to back, at about positions 855–1,470. There are also tandem arrays like the HRAS1 minisatellite: a 28-bp unit repeated ~25 times over ~700 bp, just downstream of the gene (this section uses its 25 near-perfect units, 9,390–10,089; lab 3 annotates the whole array, including degenerate copies at each end, as 9,311–10,149).

    A read that falls entirely inside a repeat could have come from any copy. Short-read assemblers collapse such repeats into one copy and break the contig at each end. The fix is a read long enough to span the whole repeat and anchor in unique sequence on both sides.

    That's why long reads, PacBio HiFi (15–25 kb, very accurate) and Oxford Nanopore (up to over 1 Mb), made the first gap-free sequence of every human autosome and the X chromosome possible: T2T-CHM13 (2022; the Y chromosome followed in 2023).

    Assemblies are compared by N50. Sort contigs longest to shortest and add up their lengths. N50 is the length of the contig at which the running total first reaches half the assembly size. Higher is more contiguous.

    Choosing a sequencing technology

    Every technology trades read length, accuracy and cost. Real genome projects often combine them: long reads to span repeats and build the scaffold, short reads or HiFi to polish the bases, and Hi-C to order contigs into chromosomes.

    TechnologyHow it readsRead lengthPer-base accuracyBest for
    SangerDye-terminator chain termination + capillary500–1,000 bp~99.99%Checking one gene or variant; confirming NGS calls
    IlluminaReversible-terminator sequencing by synthesis on clusters50–300 bp (paired)>99.9% (Q30+)Resequencing, exomes, RNA-seq, variant calling, cheapest per base
    PacBio HiFiOne polymerase watched in real time; a circular template is read many times15–25 kb~99.9%De novo assembly, structural variants, phasing haplotypes
    Oxford NanoporeIonic current changes as one strand threads through a protein pore10 kb to >1 Mb~99% (improving)Spanning huge repeats and centromeres, portable field sequencing, direct methylation

    Reference

    Key terms

    Read
    The sequence a machine reports for one DNA fragment.
    Paired-end reads
    Both ends of one fragment read, a known distance apart. The pairing helps place reads across repeats.
    Coverage (depth)
    Average number of reads covering a base: C = NL/G.
    Phred quality (Q)
    −10·log₁₀ of the error probability. Q30 means a 1 in 1,000 chance the base is wrong.
    Contig
    A gap-free stretch of assembled sequence.
    Scaffold
    Contigs ordered and oriented using pairs or long-range data, with gaps (N's) of estimated size between them.
    k-mer
    A substring of length k. A sequence of length n has n − k + 1 of them.
    De Bruijn graph
    Nodes are (k−1)-mers and edges are k-mers. An Eulerian path through it spells out the genome.
    N50
    The contig length at which contigs of that length or longer make up half the assembly.
    De novo vs reference-guided
    Building a genome from reads alone, versus aligning reads to an existing genome (the BAM read tracks you meet in lab 3’s genome browser).

    About the data

    • All sequence is the 10,309-bp human HRAS RefSeqGene entry (NG_007666.1) in this course's triplets.txt, the same entry and numbering used in all four BIO 116 labs. The coding sequence comes from the four coding exons aligned to HRAS p21 (UniProt P01112).
    • The chromatogram, flow-cell images and quality scores are simulated to show how each instrument behaves. The G12V change (c.35G>T) is a real, common HRAS hotspot mutation.
    • Reads in Sections 3 and 6 start at random positions and have no errors. The entry sits inside chromosome 11, so reads can hang off either end; only the part on the entry is drawn and counted. Real reads also carry errors, which assemblers have to correct.
