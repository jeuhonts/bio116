From a gene model to an annotated genome

In lab 2 you found where HRAS is, and in lab 3 you read its annotation in a genome browser. Annotation is everything that comes after. You mask the repeats, write the gene model in a standard file format, weigh it against the evidence, work out what the protein does, and publish a record that other people and programs can use. The seven sections follow the steps a pipeline like MAKER, BRAKER or Ensembl runs on every new genome, using the same 10,309 bases of human DNA.

    Section 1 · Structural annotation, step zero

    Mask the repeats before anything else

    About half of the human genome is repeats. Most are copies of transposable elements. The commonest is the Alu element, a ~300-bp SINE with more than a million copies. There are also simple repeats such as poly-A tracts and microsatellites like (CAAA)n.

    Repeats cause two problems for annotation. Aligners and BLAST seed on repeats and report thousands of meaningless hits. Gene finders also turn ORFs inside transposons into false exons. So the first job in every pipeline is RepeatMasker: align a library of repeat consensus sequences to the genome and mask what matches.

    This page does the same thing live. It runs a Smith–Waterman alignment of the AluY consensus against both strands of the entry, plus a scan for short tandem repeats.

    Section 2 · Writing it down

    GFF3: the gene model as nine columns

    An annotation only helps others if programs can read it. The standard is GFF3: one feature per line, nine tab-separated columns, with 1-based, inclusive coordinates (as in lab 3).

    Features form a tree through ID and Parent: a gene has an mRNA, and the mRNA has exons and CDS pieces.

    Column 8, phase, is used only on CDS lines. It is the number of bases to skip from the start of this CDS piece before the first complete codon begins:

    phase = (3 − (coding bases before this piece mod 3)) mod 3

    A colleague typed the HRAS model on the right and made four mistakes (the Parent typo also causes an error on a CDS line). The validator checks the file the way GenomeTools' gt gff3validator and NCBI's submission checks do. It also translates the CDS and compares it with UniProt P01112. Fix the file until it passes.

    Columns can be separated by tabs or, in this editor only, by two or more spaces (real validators accept only tabs). Press Tab in the editor to type a tab.

      Section 3 · Quality control

      Which model does the evidence support?

      A pipeline often ends up with several competing models for one locus: from different predictors, from RNA-seq assembly, or from protein alignments. MAKER ranks them by Annotation Edit Distance (AED). AED measures how far a model is from the evidence that overlaps it:

      SN = shared bases ÷ evidence bases
      SP = shared bases ÷ model bases
      AED = 1 − (SN + SP) ÷ 2

      AED = 0 means the model and the evidence agree base for base. AED = 1 means nothing supports it. Many projects keep only models with AED < 0.5 in the final gene set.

      Four candidate models for HRAS are drawn on the right. Two kinds of evidence are available: RNA-seq exons (from the transcript) and a protein alignment (KRAS aligned to the genome, which covers only the coding exons). Switch evidence on and off to see how the ranking changes.

      Section 4 · Functional annotation

      What is it? Homology search

      A gene model says where a gene is. Functional annotation says what it does. Almost all of it is inferred: most genes in a new genome will never be studied in a lab, so their names and functions come from similar, well-studied proteins.

      The query is the protein translated from the model in Section 2. It is searched with the Smith–Waterman algorithm, the exact local alignment that BLASTP approximates (BLOSUM62 scoring, gap existence 11 and extension 1, so a gap of k residues costs 11 + k, as in BLASTP), against a small database of well-studied human proteins. Each hit is given a bit score and an E-value:

      S′ = (λS − ln K) ÷ ln 2 bits
      E = m · n · 2−S′

      E is the number of hits this good you would expect by chance when searching a database of n residues with a query of length m. It grows with database size, so a hit that is significant against 7 proteins may not be significant against all of UniProt.

      Section 5 · Functional annotation
      Name it by orthology: the reciprocal best hit
      
  
      
    
      
      
      Section 4 showed that a best BLAST hit cannot tell HRAS from its paralogues KRAS and NRAS. A gene name should come from its orthologue, the same gene in another species, separated only by speciation.
      
      
      The simplest test for orthology is the reciprocal best hit (RBH):
      
      
        
        
      1. Forward: search your protein against the other species' proteome and take the best hit.
        2. 
        
      2. Reverse: search that hit back against your own species' proteome.
        3. 
        
      3. If the reverse best hit is the protein you started with, the pair is an RBH and very probably orthologous.
        4. 
      
      
      
      Here the other species is the house mouse. Mouse and human diverged about 90 million years ago, long after the RAS genes duplicated. So each human RAS gene should have exactly one mouse partner. The mouse proteins here are the real UniProt records. Mouse Hras and Rap1a are identical to their human counterparts. Nras differs at 3 residues, Kras at 5 and Rhoa at 1, and all but one of those differences fall in the last 25 residues, the hypervariable tail. That tail is also where the RAS paralogues differ most, although HRAS and NRAS also differ at about a dozen positions between residues 87 and 141.
      
      
      A draft annotation can be missing genes. To see how RBH protects against that, press Remove Hras from the mouse proteome.

      Section 6 · Functional annotation
      Domains, motifs and Gene Ontology terms
      
  
      
    
      
      
      Whole-protein similarity can mislead: two proteins may share one domain and nothing else. So pipelines also run InterProScan, which searches the protein against domain models (Pfam, SMART and others) and short motif patterns (PROSITE).
      
      
      HRAS has a single Ras domain (Pfam PF00071). It contains five conserved G-box motifs that bind GTP and Mg²⁺, and it ends in a CaaX box. The CaaX box is farnesylated. Together with palmitate groups on cysteines 181 and 184, these lipids hold the protein on the plasma membrane.
      
      
      A PROSITE pattern is a compact regular expression. x is any residue, x(4) is four of them, [ST] is S or T, and > anchors to the C-terminus.
      
      
      Functions are recorded as Gene Ontology (GO) terms in three aspects: molecular function (MF), biological process (BP) and cellular component (CC). Each term carries an evidence code. IDA means a direct experiment. ISS means inferred from sequence similarity and reviewed by a curator. IEA means electronic annotation, assigned by a pipeline with no curator.

      Section 7 · Release
      Publish the record and grade the gene set
      
  
      
    
      
      
      The finished annotation combines everything: repeat features, the gene model, and functional attributes (name, product, database cross-references, GO terms). The record on the right is built from your work in Sections 1–6. If your GFF3 still has errors, the reference model is used and a warning is shown.

It searches the annotation for a set of genes that are present in a single copy in almost every species of a lineage (3,354 for vertebrates, 255 for all eukaryotes).       Reference
      Key terms
      
  
      
    
      Structural annotation
      Where the features are: repeats, genes, exons, UTRs, CDS.
      
    
      Functional annotation
      What the features do: names, products, domains, GO terms, pathways.
      
    
      Soft / hard masking
      Marking repeats as lowercase letters (still usable for extending alignments) or replacing them with N (gone for good).
      
    
      SINE / Alu
      Short interspersed nuclear element. Alu is the primate SINE, ~300 bp, with a poly-A tail; about 10% of the human genome.
      
    
      GFF3
      Nine-column, tab-separated, 1-based format for genome features, linked into trees by ID and Parent.
      
    
      CDS phase
      Bases to skip at the start of a CDS piece to reach the first full codon (0, 1 or 2).
      
    
      AED
      Annotation Edit Distance: 1 − (SN + SP)/2 between a model and its evidence. 0 is perfect support.
      
    
      E-value
      Expected number of chance hits at least this good, given the query and database sizes.
      
    
      Orthologue / paralogue
      Genes related by speciation (the same gene in two species) or by duplication (e.g. HRAS, KRAS, NRAS).
      
    
      Reciprocal best hit
      Two genes in different species that are each other’s best search hit. A quick, widely used test for 1:1 orthology.
      
    
      PROSITE pattern
      A short motif written as a regular expression, e.g. G-x(4)-G-K-[ST], a simplified version of PROSITE's P-loop pattern PS00017 ([AG]-x(4)-G-K-[ST]).
      
    
      GO evidence code
      How a GO term was assigned: IDA (direct assay), ISS (sequence similarity, curated), IEA (electronic, uncurated), and others.
      
    
      BUSCO
      Benchmarking Universal Single-Copy Orthologs: the share of expected conserved genes an assembly or annotation contains.
