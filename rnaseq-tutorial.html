Stage 1 of 5

Experimental design

The decisions you can't fix later

Gene expression varies between individuals even under identical conditions. To tell a treatment effect from that background, you need biological replicates: independent animals, patients or cultures. Sequencing the same library twice (a technical replicate) only measures the sequencer's noise, which is small compared with biological variation.

For most experiments, more replicates buy more power than more reads per sample. ENCODE guidelines suggest at least 2–3 replicates and about 20–30 million reads each for standard gene-level analysis.

Stage 2 of 5

RNA library preparation

Getting rid of the RNA you don't want

About 80–90% of the RNA in a cell is ribosomal RNA. Sequencing total RNA directly would waste most reads on rRNA, so libraries first enrich the RNA of interest. Poly(A) selection uses oligo-dT beads to pull out RNAs with a poly(A) tail, which is most mature mRNA. rRNA depletion removes rRNA with probes and keeps everything else, including pre-mRNA, many lncRNAs and non-polyadenylated histone mRNAs.

RNA is then fragmented and reverse-transcribed into cDNA, and the cDNA gets adapters as in Part 1. Stranded protocols (dUTP marking of the second strand) record which strand each transcript came from, which matters where genes overlap on opposite strands.

Stage 3 of 5

Spliced alignment

Reads that jump over introns

Mature mRNA has had its introns spliced out, so a read from an exon–exon junction lands on two pieces of the genome that may be thousands of bases apart. A DNA aligner such as BWA can't split a read like that. Splice-aware aligners (STAR, HISAT2) can, recording the jump as an N in the CIGAR string, for example 60M1850N40M.

Junction reads also reveal alternative splicing. If an exon is sometimes skipped, some reads join the flanking exons directly. The share of transcripts that include the exon is its percent spliced in (PSI, Ψ). Pseudoaligners such as Salmon and kallisto skip base-level alignment and assign reads straight to transcripts, which is much faster for counting.

Stage 4 of 5

Counting and normalization

Why raw counts can't be compared directly

Tools like featureCounts or HTSeq count the reads overlapping each gene, giving a count matrix of genes by samples. Raw counts depend on more than expression. A sample sequenced twice as deeply has twice the counts. A long gene collects more reads than a short one at the same molar level. And if one gene takes a huge share of reads in one sample, every other gene in that sample gets fewer.

CPM and TPM

CPM divides by total reads (depth). TPM also divides by gene length first, so it is used to compare different genes within a sample. Neither corrects for composition.

Median of ratios (DESeq2) and TMM (edgeR)

Assume most genes don't change. Each sample gets a size factor from the typical ratio of its counts to the other samples, so a few very highly expressed genes can't skew it.

Stage 5 of 5

Differential expression

Twenty thousand tests, and the false positives that come with them

For each gene, a differential expression tool (DESeq2, edgeR, limma-voom) estimates the log2 fold change between groups and a p-value. Counts are modelled with the negative binomial distribution, whose variance grows faster than the mean, matching the extra variability between biological replicates.

Testing thousands of genes at p < 0.05 guarantees false positives: 5% of the genes that don't change will pass by chance. The Benjamini–Hochberg procedure controls the false discovery rate (FDR) instead, the expected share of false hits among the genes you call significant. The adjusted p-value is often called padj or q.

Wrap-up

Bulk and single-cell RNA-seq

An average over millions of cells, or a profile of each one

Bulk RNA-seq

  • One expression profile per sample, averaged over all its cells
  • 20–40 million reads per sample; detects low-abundance genes well
  • Well-established statistics (DESeq2, edgeR, limma)
  • A change in cell-type mix can look like a change in expression
  • Best for condition comparisons with many replicates

Single-cell RNA-seq

  • Thousands to millions of cells, each with its own barcode
  • Unique molecular identifiers (UMIs) count molecules, not PCR copies
  • Sparse: only 10–20% of each cell's mRNA is captured
  • Clustering and UMAP reveal cell types and states
  • Treat donors, not cells, as replicates (pseudobulk) for DE tests

Modern frontiers

  • Cell atlases. The Human Cell Atlas and Tabula Sapiens map every cell type across human tissues with single-cell RNA-seq.
  • Spatial transcriptomics. Visium, Xenium and MERFISH measure expression while keeping each cell's position in the tissue.
  • Full-length isoforms. PacBio Kinnex/Iso-Seq and nanopore direct RNA sequencing read whole transcripts, so isoforms no longer have to be inferred from short fragments. Direct RNA reads can also detect modifications such as m6A.
  • Fast quantification. Pseudoalignment (Salmon, kallisto) quantifies a sample in minutes on a laptop, making re-analysis of public data practical at scale.

Simulations use simplified models for teaching: power uses a normal approximation on the log scale, read composition values are typical rather than measured, and the volcano plot uses negative binomial counts tested with a t-test on log counts instead of a full DESeq2 fit.
