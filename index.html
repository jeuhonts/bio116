    The biology you need before you write your first alignment

    Bioinformatics treats living systems as data. This primer walks through the biology behind that data, from the cell down to a single base, and every idea comes with something you can try. Chapter 15 runs real analyses on the course sequence file, triplets.txt.

    • Treat this as a fast review. Skim the prose and try the tools.
    • Pink CS lens boxes show how a programmer sees each idea. They are the vocabulary your code will use.

    If you studied computer science

    • Read the chapters in order. Each one builds on the last.
    • Green Bio depth boxes add detail that biologists take for granted.

    CS lens

    A genome is a string over the alphabet Σ = {A, C, G, T}. The human one is about 3.1 × 10⁹ characters per copy. At 2 bits per base that is roughly 775 MB, which is smaller than many video games. Much of bioinformatics comes down to string algorithms, statistics and databases, applied to strings that were produced by evolution and read by noisy machines.

    Chapter 01

    Cells, and the scale of things

    Every sequence you analyse came out of a cell. It helps to know where in the cell it came from and how big the pieces are.

    Zoom ladder

    Click a level to zoom from a whole person down to one base pair.

    Prokaryotes

    Bacteria and archaea

    • No nucleus. DNA sits in the cytoplasm in a region called the nucleoid.
    • Usually one circular chromosome, plus small circular plasmids.
    • Genes are packed densely, often ~88% coding, and have few or no introns.
    • Related genes are often grouped in operons and transcribed together.
    • Transcription and translation happen at the same time and in the same place.

    Eukaryotes

    Animals, plants, fungi, protists

    • DNA is enclosed in a nucleus and wrapped around histones as chromatin.
    • Several linear chromosomes with telomeres at the ends.
    • Genes are split into exons and introns. Only ~1–2% of the human genome codes for protein.
    • Transcripts are processed (capped, spliced, polyadenylated) before they leave the nucleus.
    • Mitochondria, and chloroplasts in plants, carry their own small genomes.

    Nearly every cell in your body carries the same genome. A neuron and a liver cell differ because they express different genes: they make RNA and protein from different parts of the same instructions. That is why bioinformatics works at several layers, each with its own data type and measuring technology.

    Bio depth

    The human mitochondrial genome is a 16,569 bp circle with 37 genes. It is inherited only from the mother and has no introns, which is why it is used in ancestry studies and forensics. Each cell holds hundreds to thousands of copies of it.

    CS lens

    Think of the genome as source code that every cell has a full copy of. Gene expression is closer to which functions actually get called in a given process. Two cells with identical "code" can behave very differently, so knowing the sequence alone rarely tells you what a cell is doing.

    Chapter 02

    DNA, the molecule

    Four letters, two strands, one direction. Most sequence-handling bugs come from forgetting one of these three facts.

    A DNA strand is a chain of nucleotides. Each nucleotide has a sugar (deoxyribose), a phosphate and one of four bases: Adenine, Cytosine, Guanine and Thymine. The sugar–phosphate backbone has a direction. One end is called 5′ (five-prime) and the other 3′, after the carbon atoms of the sugar. Sequences are always written 5′ → 3′.

    Two strands pair up into the double helix. A pairs with T through two hydrogen bonds, and G pairs with C through three. The strands run in opposite directions, which is called antiparallel. So the partner of a strand is its reverse complement: complement every base, then read backwards.

    Numbers worth remembering

    • Rise per base pair: 0.34 nm. About 10.5 bp per helical turn.
    • Helix diameter: about 2 nm.
    • Stretched out, the DNA in one human cell is about 2 m long.
    • G·C pairs have three hydrogen bonds and stack more strongly, so GC-rich DNA melts at a higher temperature.
    • Purines (A, G) have two rings. Pyrimidines (C, T, U) have one.

    IUPAC ambiguity codes

    Used in FASTA files, primers and motifs when a position is not a single base.

    CS lens

    Reverse complement is the most common operation in the field, and forgetting it is the most common bug. A read from a sequencer can come from either strand. A motif search that only scans the given string misses half the genome. Watch out for reverse palindromes like GAATTC, whose reverse complement is the same string. Many restriction enzymes recognise exactly these.

    Bio depth

    RNA uses ribose instead of deoxyribose and uracil (U) in place of thymine. It is usually single-stranded and folds back on itself into hairpins and loops. In sequence files, RNA is often still written with T.

    Chapter 03

    Chromosomes: packing two metres into a nucleus

    A chromosome is one very long DNA molecule plus the proteins that fold it. How it is folded affects which genes can be read, and chromosome names and coordinates show up in every genomics file.

    Levels of packing

    Step from bare DNA to a condensed metaphase chromosome.

    Euchromatin

    Open and active

    • Loosely packed. Transcription factors and RNA polymerase can reach the DNA.
    • Rich in genes. Histone tails are often acetylated.
    • Shows up as open-chromatin peaks in ATAC-seq data.

    Heterochromatin

    Compact and quiet

    • Tightly packed and mostly silent.
    • Constitutive heterochromatin is always packed: centromeres, telomeres, many repeats.
    • Facultative heterochromatin is packed only in some cells, like the inactive X chromosome in female cells.

    Band address decoder

    Genes are often located by cytogenetic band, like CFTR at 7q31.2.

    Counting chromosomes

    Human body cells are diploid: 23 pairs, 46 chromosomes. Pairs 1–22 are autosomes, numbered roughly from largest to smallest. The 23rd pair is the sex chromosomes, XX or XY. Eggs and sperm are haploid, with 23. A karyotype is written as the count, then the sex chromosomes, then any changes: 46,XX is typical, and 47,XY,+21 is a male with trisomy 21 (Down syndrome).

    During meiosis, each pair of matching chromosomes swaps segments. This is called crossing over or recombination. Genes close together on a chromosome are usually inherited together. That linkage is what makes genetic mapping and genome-wide association studies (GWAS) possible.

    CS lens

    Chromosome names are not standard across databases. UCSC calls chromosome 1 chr1 and the mitochondrial genome chrM. Ensembl and NCBI use 1 and MT. Mixing a BAM file from one with an annotation file from the other silently returns zero overlaps. Reference files also contain extra sequences, such as chrUn_… (unplaced) and _alt haplotypes, which most pipelines need to handle or filter.

    Bio depth

    Telomeres are thousands of copies of TTAGGG that shorten with each division unless the enzyme telomerase extends them. Human centromeres are built from a 171 bp repeat called α-satellite, repeated over millions of bases. Both regions were missing from the reference genome until the telomere-to-telomere (T2T) assembly in 2022.

    Chapter 04

    Genomes and genes

    A genome is all the DNA of an organism. Genes are the parts that get transcribed into RNA, and they are a small share of the whole in humans.

    Genome size is not complexity

    Approximate haploid genome sizes, log scale. Hover a bar for gene counts.

    A lungfish or an onion has far more DNA than you do, and roughly as many protein-coding genes. Most of the difference is repetitive, non-coding DNA. This mismatch is called the C-value paradox.

    Anatomy of a eukaryotic gene

    Click a part of the gene to see what it does.
    Promoter. Click any part of the gene.

    What the rest of the genome is made of

    Roughly half of the human genome is repeated sequence. The most abundant single family is the Alu element, a ~300 bp sequence present in over a million copies and making up about 10% of our DNA. Alus copy themselves through an RNA intermediate and end in a poly-A tail. You will find two of them in the course sequence in Chapter 15.

    • Transposable elements (Alu, LINE-1, endogenous retroviruses): sequences that copy and paste themselves around the genome.
    • Tandem repeats: short units repeated head to tail. Microsatellites have 1–6 bp units, minisatellites 10–60 bp. They vary in length between people, which makes them useful in forensics.
    • Regulatory DNA: promoters, enhancers and insulators that control when and where genes are switched on.
    • Non-coding RNA genes: rRNA, tRNA, microRNAs, long non-coding RNAs. They are transcribed but never translated.
    • Pseudogenes: broken copies of genes that no longer make a working protein.
    CS lens

    Repeats are the main reason genome assembly is hard. If a repeat is longer than your reads, you cannot tell which copy a read came from, just as you cannot rebuild a text from shreds shorter than its duplicated paragraphs. This is why long-read sequencing was needed to finish the human genome (the T2T assembly, 2022).

    Bio depth

    Genome coordinates depend on the reference assembly. Human position chr7:117,559,590 means different bases in GRCh37 (hg19) and GRCh38 (hg38). Always check which build a file uses before you compare coordinates.

    Chapter 05

    The central dogma: DNA → RNA → protein

    Information flows from DNA to RNA by transcription, and from RNA to protein by translation. Step through one toy gene to watch it happen.

    From gene to protein

    A made-up gene with two exons and one intron.

    Reading frames

    The ribosome reads mRNA three bases at a time, starting at a start codon (almost always AUG). Which of the three possible starting offsets it uses is the reading frame. Counting both strands, any stretch of DNA has six reading frames: three forward and three on the reverse complement. An open reading frame (ORF) is a stretch that starts with ATG and runs to the next stop codon without interruption.

    Frame shifter

    Same DNA, six different readings. Green is a start codon, red is a stop.

    CS lens

    Translation is a lookup table applied to non-overlapping 3-character chunks: protein = "".join(CODE[dna[i:i+3]] for i in range(f, len(dna)-2, 3)). Six-frame translation is that loop run with offsets 0, 1, 2 on the string and on its reverse complement. Gene finders build on this, then add statistics about codon usage and splice sites.

    Bio depth

    The template strand is the one RNA polymerase reads. The mRNA is complementary to the template, so it has the same sequence as the coding (sense) strand, with U for T. Databases and gene annotations report the coding strand. "The gene is on the minus strand" means its coding strand is the reverse complement of the reference.

    Chapter 06

    The genetic code

    64 codons map to 20 amino acids and a stop signal. The code is redundant, and that redundancy has a structure.

    Standard codon table

    Click a codon to highlight its synonyms. Rows are the first base, columns the second, and the third base varies inside each cell.
    Highlight:
    Click any codon.

    Patterns to notice

    • 1 start codon (AUG, methionine) and 3 stops (UAA, UAG, UGA).
    • Most redundancy is in the third position. Changing it is often synonymous: the amino acid stays the same. This third-position flexibility is called wobble.
    • Leucine, serine and arginine each have six codons. Methionine and tryptophan have only one.
    • Similar codons tend to code for chemically similar amino acids, so many point mutations are mild.

    "Standard" is not universal

    • Vertebrate mitochondria read UGA as tryptophan and AGA/AGG as stop.
    • Some ciliates read UAA and UAG as glutamine.
    • NCBI numbers these variants as translation tables. Table 1 is standard, table 2 is vertebrate mitochondrial, table 11 is bacterial.
    • Selenocysteine (U) and pyrrolysine (O) are the 21st and 22nd amino acids, inserted at special stop codons.
    CS lens

    The code is a map from 4³ = 64 keys to 21 values, so it is not invertible. Going from protein back to DNA ("back-translation") gives many possible answers. Which codons an organism prefers (codon usage bias) varies by species, and that bias is a feature gene-prediction tools use.

    Chapter 07

    Gene expression and regulation

    Every cell carries the same genes, but each one uses only some of them. Regulation decides which genes are on, how strongly, and when. Most omics data measures the result of that regulation.

    A bacterial switch: the lac operon

    E. coli only makes lactose-digesting enzymes when lactose is present and glucose is scarce.

    A eukaryotic gene's control panel

    A simplified cartoon. Turn each layer on or off and watch the output.

    Transcription factors are proteins that recognise short DNA motifs, typically 6–12 bp, like the TATA box you met in Chapter 05 or the CACGTG "E-box". Humans have about 1,600 of them. Enhancers are binding sites for activators that can sit tens or hundreds of kilobases from the gene they control. The DNA loops round so the enhancer touches the promoter. This is why a variant far from any gene can still change how a gene is expressed.

    Alternative splicing

    One gene, several proteins. Include or skip the middle exons.

    About 95% of human multi-exon genes are alternatively spliced. Each distinct mRNA is called an isoform or transcript, and annotation files list them separately.

    Measuring expression: why RNA-seq counts need normalising

    Toy data: four genes in two samples. Switch the unit and read the fold changes.
    CS lens

    An RNA-seq experiment ends as a matrix: genes as rows, samples as columns, read counts in the cells. Differential expression tools such as DESeq2 and edgeR fit a statistical model (negative binomial) to those counts. They need biological replicates, several samples per condition, to tell real change from noise. A fold change without replicates is an anecdote. Reported results are usually log₂ fold change plus a p-value adjusted for testing ~20,000 genes at once.

    Bio depth

    Transcription factor motifs are fuzzy. They are described as a position weight matrix: for each position, how often each base appears in known binding sites. Databases such as JASPAR store thousands of them, drawn as sequence logos. A 6 bp motif occurs by chance about once every 4 kb on each strand, so most matches in a genome are never bound. Chromatin accessibility data helps separate real sites from chance.

    Chapter 08

    Proteins: structure and function

    A protein is a chain of amino acids that folds into a 3D shape. The shape decides what it does, and the chemistry of each residue decides the shape.

    The backbone

    • Amino acids join by peptide bonds, giving a backbone that repeats N–Cα–C.
    • Each residue hangs its side chain (R) off the Cα. The side chains differ; the backbone does not.
    • Chains have direction: the N-terminus is made first and written first; the C-terminus is last.
    • Typical proteins are 100–1,000 residues. The median human protein is roughly 400.

    Why it folds

    • Hydrophobic effect: oily side chains cluster away from water, forming the core. This is the main driving force.
    • Hydrogen bonds between backbone groups form helices and sheets.
    • Salt bridges between + and − side chains, and disulfide bonds between cysteines, add stability.
    • The sequence alone is usually enough to specify the fold (Anfinsen's experiment, 1961). Some proteins need helper chaperones to fold correctly.
    • About a third of human proteins have intrinsically disordered regions that have no fixed shape.

    Domains: proteins are built from parts

    Most proteins are made of domains: compact units, typically 50–250 residues, that fold on their own and often have a job of their own, such as binding DNA or adding phosphate groups. Evolution reuses domains by shuffling them into new combinations. Recognising a known domain in a new sequence is often the fastest way to guess its function. Pfam and InterPro catalogue these families.

    What proteins do

    Catalysis

    Enzymes

    Speed up reactions, often millions of times. The substrate binds a pocket called the active site. Example: DNA polymerase, lactase.

    Transport

    Carriers and channels

    Move molecules around the body or across membranes. Example: haemoglobin carries O₂; aquaporins let water through membranes.

    Signalling

    Receptors and kinases

    Detect signals and pass them on, often by adding phosphate groups. Example: EGFR, insulin receptor.

    Regulation

    Transcription factors

    Bind DNA and switch genes on or off. Example: p53 activates repair and cell-death genes after DNA damage.

    Structure

    Scaffolds and fibres

    Give cells and tissues shape and strength. Example: collagen, keratin, actin.

    Defence

    Antibodies

    Recognise foreign molecules with variable loops that differ from one antibody to the next.

    Movement

    Motor proteins

    Turn chemical energy (ATP) into motion. Example: myosin in muscle, kinesin carrying cargo along microtubules.

    Storage and hormones

    Messengers

    Carry signals between cells or store nutrients. Example: insulin, ferritin (stores iron).

    Enzyme kinetics

    Michaelis–Menten: how reaction speed depends on substrate concentration.

    After translation: modifications

    • Phosphorylation of S, T or Y: an on/off switch added by kinases, removed by phosphatases.
    • Glycosylation: sugar chains, common on secreted and cell-surface proteins.
    • Ubiquitination of K: tags a protein for destruction by the proteasome.
    • Cleavage: signal peptides and pro-segments are cut off. Insulin loses its signal peptide and C-peptide.
    • Acetylation and methylation of K: important on histones (Chapters 03 and 07).

    Address labels in the sequence

    • Signal peptide at the N-terminus: a hydrophobic stretch that sends a protein for secretion or to a membrane.
    • Nuclear localisation signal: a short basic stretch such as PKKKRKV (from the SV40 virus).
    • Transmembrane helices: ~20 hydrophobic residues that span the membrane.
    • ER retention: KDEL at the C-terminus keeps a protein in the endoplasmic reticulum.
    • Tools like SignalP and DeepTMHMM predict these from sequence alone.

    Structure explains disease: sickle-cell haemoglobin

    In Chapter 10 you can make the single change c.20A>T in the β-globin gene. It swaps a charged glutamate on the protein's surface for a hydrophobic valine. That valine creates a sticky patch. When oxygen is low, haemoglobin molecules stick to each other through it and form long fibres, which bend red cells into a sickle shape. One base changes one residue, the residue changes a surface, and the surface changes a whole cell.

    Bio depth

    Km is the substrate concentration at which an enzyme runs at half its top speed. A low Km usually means the enzyme binds its substrate tightly. Competitive inhibitors compete for the active site, so enough substrate outcompetes them: Vmax is unchanged but Km appears larger. Many drugs work this way, such as statins inhibiting HMG-CoA reductase.

    Chapter 09

    Metabolism: chemistry run by enzymes

    Metabolism is every chemical reaction in a cell. Enzymes from the last chapter carry out the reactions, genes encode the enzymes, and pathways chain the reactions together into a network.

    Catabolism

    Breaking down

    • Splits food molecules (sugars, fats, proteins) into smaller ones.
    • Releases energy and captures it as ATP, and as electrons carried by NADH and FADH₂.
    • Example: glucose → CO₂ + H₂O in cellular respiration.

    Anabolism

    Building up

    • Builds large molecules (proteins, DNA, lipids) from small ones.
    • Spends ATP for energy and NADPH for electrons.
    • Example: amino acids → protein at the ribosome.

    ATP (adenosine triphosphate) is the cell's energy currency. Breaking off its last phosphate releases about 30 kJ/mol under standard conditions, and a lot more under real cell conditions. A resting adult turns over roughly their own body weight in ATP every day, recycling the same small pool thousands of times.

    CS lens

    A metabolic network is a graph: metabolites are nodes, reactions are edges (really hyperedges, since one reaction can take several inputs). Databases such as KEGG, Reactome and MetaCyc store these maps. Flux balance analysis writes the whole network as a stoichiometric matrix S and finds reaction rates v with S·v = 0 (nothing piles up) that maximise some goal, such as growth. That is a linear programming problem. It is used to predict which gene knockouts will kill a bacterium or make it produce more of a chemical.

    Bio depth

    Most mammals stop making lactase after weaning. In many people of European, East African and Middle Eastern ancestry, variants in an enhancer upstream of the lactase gene LCT keep it switched on into adulthood. The best known is −13910C>T, which sits inside the neighbouring gene MCM6. It is a textbook case of a regulatory variant (Chapter 07) under recent natural selection. Metabolomics measures hundreds to thousands of small molecules at once, mostly by mass spectrometry.

    Chapter 11

    Evolution, homology and alignment

    Sequences are similar because they share an ancestor. Alignment is how we measure that similarity, and it is the core algorithm of the field.

    Words that are often misused

    • Homologous: sharing a common ancestor. This is yes-or-no. "70% homologous" is wrong. Say "70% identical".
    • Orthologs: homologs split by a speciation event, such as human and mouse haemoglobin β. They usually keep the same function.
    • Paralogs: homologs split by a gene duplication in one genome, such as human haemoglobin α and β. They often take on new functions.
    • Identity and similarity are measured from an alignment. Homology is inferred from them.

    Why alignment works

    • Mutations build up over time, so sequences drift apart slowly.
    • Parts that matter for function change less because natural selection removes harmful changes. Similarity survives there longest.
    • An alignment places the sequences so that bases descended from the same ancestral base line up, with gaps for insertions and deletions.
    • Score matrices such as BLOSUM62 reward substitutions that are common in real protein families.

    Dynamic-programming aligner

    Needleman–Wunsch (global) or Smith–Waterman (local). Up to 16 bases each.

    CS lens

    This is edit distance with weights. Each cell F(i,j) is the best score for aligning the first i characters of one string with the first j of the other. It takes the max of a diagonal step (match or mismatch), a step down, or a step right (gaps). That costs O(nm) time and memory. BLAST exists because O(nm) against a database of billions of residues is too slow: it finds short exact "seed" matches first and only extends around them.

    Bio depth

    Choose global alignment for sequences you expect to be related end to end, such as two orthologous genes. Choose local alignment to find a shared domain or motif inside otherwise different sequences, or to place a read on a chromosome. BLAST reports local alignments with an E-value: the number of hits this good you would expect by chance in a database of that size.

    Chapter 12

    Phylogenetics: reading and building trees

    A phylogenetic tree is a hypothesis about how sequences or species are related through common ancestors. You will read hundreds of them, so learn to read them correctly first.

    Parts of a tree

    • Tips (leaves): the sequences or species you sampled, usually living today.
    • Internal nodes: inferred common ancestors. They were not observed.
    • Root: the common ancestor of everything in the tree. It gives the direction of time.
    • Branch lengths: in a phylogram, the amount of change, usually substitutions per site. In a cladogram, only the branching order is shown.
    • Clade: an ancestor and all of its descendants. Also called a monophyletic group.

    Common misreadings

    • Tips drawn next to each other are not necessarily closest relatives. Relatedness is set by the most recent common ancestor (MRCA).
    • Branches can rotate around any node without changing the tree, like a mobile.
    • No living species is "more evolved". Human and chimpanzee have had exactly the same time to evolve since their split.
    • An unrooted tree shows relationships but not direction. Adding an outgroup, a sequence known to lie outside the group, lets you place the root.

    How trees are built

    1. Collect homologous sequences and align them (Chapter 11). A tree is only as good as its alignment.
    2. Pick a method. Distance methods (UPGMA, neighbour joining) turn the alignment into a table of pairwise distances and cluster it. Character methods look at every column: maximum parsimony finds the tree needing the fewest changes, maximum likelihood (IQ-TREE, RAxML) and Bayesian methods (MrBayes, BEAST) use an explicit model of how sequences change.
    3. Measure support. Bootstrapping resamples alignment columns with replacement, rebuilds the tree many times, and reports how often each clade reappears. Values above about 70–95% (depending on the method) are usually taken as good support.
    4. Root it with an outgroup, or with a molecular clock.

    The tree of life

    • All cellular life falls into Bacteria, Archaea and eukaryotes. Carl Woese defined this split in 1977 by comparing 16S/18S ribosomal RNA.
    • Recent genome data place eukaryotes within the Archaea, close to the Asgard archaea.
    • Mitochondria descend from an engulfed alphaproteobacterium, and chloroplasts from a cyanobacterium (endosymbiosis). Their genomes still group with those bacteria on trees.
    • Genes also move sideways between bacteria by horizontal gene transfer, which is how antibiotic resistance spreads. This makes the bacterial "tree" partly a web.

    What trees are used for

    • Outbreak tracking: SARS-CoV-2 lineages and transmission chains were followed in near real time with trees (Nextstrain).
    • Ortholog and paralog calling: a gene tree reconciled with the species tree shows which splits were speciations and which were duplications (Chapter 11).
    • Metagenomics: placing 16S rRNA reads on a reference tree identifies which microbes are in a sample.
    • Dating: with fossils or sampling dates as calibration, a molecular clock converts branch lengths into years.
    CS lens

    A rooted phylogeny is a binary tree, and Newick is its serialisation: nested parentheses, commas between children, optional :length after each node, ending in ;. The number of distinct rooted binary trees for n tips is (2n−3)!!. That is 10,395 trees for 7 tips and about 8 × 10²¹ for 20, so exhaustive search is impossible and tree building uses heuristics. Maximum parsimony scoring on a fixed tree uses the Fitch algorithm, a bottom-up pass of set intersections and unions.

    Bio depth

    The p-distance, the fraction of sites that differ, underestimates true divergence. Once sequences have diverged a lot, a site can change twice (A→G→A) and look unchanged. The Jukes–Cantor correction, d = −¾ ln(1 − 4p/3), estimates the hidden changes. It tends to infinity as p approaches 0.75, the level at which two random DNA sequences would match by chance. More realistic models (K80, HKY, GTR) allow transitions and transversions to happen at different rates.

    Anatomy of FASTA and FASTQ

    Hover or tap a quality character to decode it.

    FASTA: sequence only

    A header line starting with >, then sequence lines. Used for references, genes and proteins.

    FASTQ: sequence plus quality

    Four lines per read: @header, bases, +, and one quality character per base.

    Quality is stored as Phred scores: Q = −10·log₁₀(Perror). Each character's ASCII code minus 33 gives Q. Hover a quality character.

    Coverage calculator

    How much sequencing do you need? Based on the Lander–Waterman model.

    Reads land at random, so coverage varies from base to base. With mean depth c, the chance that a given base gets no reads at all is about e−c. Real data is less even than this because GC-rich and repetitive regions are harder to sequence and map.

    The pipeline in one breath

    1. Extract DNA or RNA, fragment it, add adapters: a library.
    2. Sequence it: FASTQ files of reads.
    3. Check quality and trim adapters and poor bases.
    4. Align reads to a reference (BAM) or assemble them from scratch.
    5. Call variants (VCF), count genes, or find peaks, depending on the question.
    6. Interpret with annotations and databases.

    Databases you will use

    • GenBank / NCBI: nucleotide sequences, BLAST, PubMed.
    • UniProt: curated protein sequences and functions.
    • RCSB PDB: experimental 3D structures.
    • Ensembl and the UCSC Genome Browser: annotated genomes.
    • ClinVar and gnomAD: clinical and population variants.
    CS lens

    Short-read alignment tools such as BWA and Bowtie2 index the reference with a Burrows–Wheeler transform and FM-index. That lets them find where a 150-character read occurs in a 3-billion-character string in time proportional to the read length, not the genome length. Assemblers build de Bruijn graphs from k-mers or overlap graphs from long reads.

    Limits to keep in mind

    • Variants of uncertain significance (VUS) are often the most common result of a clinical test. They should not change care, and many are later reclassified as benign.
    • Penetrance: carrying a disease variant does not guarantee disease. Many "pathogenic" variants are found in healthy people.
    • Consumer tests usually use SNP arrays that read ~600,000 chosen sites, not the whole genome. Their calls of very rare variants are often false positives and need confirming in a clinical lab.
    • Short reads miss parts of the genome: long repeats, segmental duplications and some genes with near-identical copies.
    • A genome is fixed at birth, but most common diseases also depend heavily on environment, lifestyle and chance.

    Privacy and ethics

    • Your genome is also partly your relatives' genome. Sharing yours reveals information about people who did not consent.
    • Genomes cannot be truly anonymised. Since 2018, police have identified suspects by matching crime-scene DNA to distant relatives in genealogy databases.
    • In the US, GINA (2008) bans genetic discrimination in health insurance and employment, but not in life, disability or long-term-care insurance. Laws differ by country.
    • Clinical sequencing can turn up secondary findings unrelated to the reason for testing. People should decide in advance whether they want to know.
    • Reference genomes and databases under-represent most of the world's populations, which makes interpretation less accurate for many people.
    CS lens

    A standard germline pipeline: FASTQ (~100 GB at 30× coverage) → align with BWA-MEM → sorted, duplicate-marked BAM → call variants with GATK HaplotypeCaller or DeepVariant → VCF → annotate each variant with its gene and consequence (Ensembl VEP), population frequency (gnomAD) and clinical reports (ClinVar) → filter. In the VCF genotype field, 0/1 means unphased and 0|1 phased: the bar says which chromosome copy carries the alternative allele. Phasing matters when two variants hit the same gene. If they are on different copies, no working copy is left.

    Bio depth

    Each child carries about 60–70 new mutations absent from both parents. Roughly 80% arise in the father, and the number rises by about 1–2 for each extra year of the father's age, because sperm precursors keep dividing throughout life. Clinical labs classify variants on a five-tier scale set by the ACMG: pathogenic, likely pathogenic, uncertain significance, likely benign and benign. The evidence includes population frequency, predicted effect, segregation in families and functional tests.
