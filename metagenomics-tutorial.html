Who lives here?

A gram of stool or soil holds billions of microbes from hundreds to thousands of species, most of which have never been grown in a lab. Metagenomics sequences them all at once. Follow a gut microbiome study from choosing an assay to recovering whole genomes out of the mixture. Each stage has a model to play with and two checkpoint questions.

Stage 1 of 5

Amplicon or shotgun

Barcode one gene, or sequence everything

16S rRNA amplicon sequencing

PCR-amplify part of the 16S ribosomal RNA gene, which every bacterium and archaeon carries. Cheap (a few thousand reads per sample is enough), works on low-biomass samples, and has large reference databases (SILVA, GTDB). Tells you who is there, usually to genus level, but not what genes they carry. PCR primers favour some groups over others.

Shotgun metagenomics

Fragment and sequence all DNA in the sample. Detects bacteria, archaea, viruses, fungi and host DNA; gives species or strain resolution; and reveals genes and pathways such as antibiotic resistance. Needs millions of reads per sample, costs more, and host DNA can swamp microbial reads in tissue samples.

The 16S gene is about 1,540 bp. Nine hypervariable regions (V1–V9) differ between taxa and are what let you tell groups apart. The conserved stretches between them are where universal PCR primers can bind.

Stage 2 of 5

OTUs and ASVs

Is that a new species, or a sequencing error?

Every amplicon read carries a few errors, so a sample with 100 real sequence variants can yield tens of thousands of distinct reads. The older fix was to cluster reads into operational taxonomic units (OTUs) at 97% identity, a rough stand-in for species. It absorbs errors but also merges genuinely different organisms.

Modern tools (DADA2, Deblur, UNOISE) denoise instead. They learn the run's error rates and ask whether a rare sequence close to an abundant one is better explained as an error of it. What remains are amplicon sequence variants (ASVs), exact sequences that differ by as little as one base and can be compared directly between studies.

Stage 3 of 5

Classifying shotgun reads

k-mers, a taxonomy tree and the lowest common ancestor

A shotgun run gives tens of millions of short reads from thousands of genomes, and aligning each one to every reference is too slow. Kraken2 instead cuts each read into k-mers (35 bp by default) and looks each up in a database. Each k-mer is stored under the lowest common ancestor (LCA) of all genomes that contain it: a k-mer found only in E. coli points to that species, while one shared by all Bacteroides points to the genus.

The read goes to the deepest node on the root-to-leaf path with the most k-mer hits. A confidence threshold then demands that a minimum share of the read's k-mers support that node or its descendants. If they don't, the call moves up the tree. Bracken then turns read counts into species abundance estimates.

Stage 4 of 5

Diversity and composition

How many kinds, and how do samples differ?

Alpha diversity describes one sample: richness (how many taxa), and indices such as Shannon and Simpson that also weigh evenness. Observed richness depends on sequencing depth, since rare taxa only appear once you read deeply enough. A rarefaction curve shows that dependence.

Beta diversity compares samples. Bray–Curtis dissimilarity uses abundances; Jaccard uses presence and absence only. Principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) lays out the distance matrix in two dimensions, and PERMANOVA tests whether groups differ.

Sequencing gives relative abundances: every sample adds up to 100%. That makes the data compositional. If one taxon blooms, every other taxon's share falls even if its absolute numbers haven't changed. Methods such as ANCOM-BC and ALDEx2 account for this, and spike-ins or qPCR of total bacterial load can recover absolute abundance.

Genomes from mixtures

Binning contigs into metagenome-assembled genomes

Assembling a metagenome (MEGAHIT, metaSPAdes, or metaFlye for long reads) gives contigs from many genomes jumbled together. Binning groups contigs that came from the same genome using two signals. Sequence composition (GC content, tetranucleotide frequencies) is similar across one genome. Coverage is similar too, because every part of a genome is present in the same number of copies. Coverage across several samples, called differential coverage, is especially powerful.

Each bin is a metagenome-assembled genome (MAG). CheckM2 estimates its quality from ~100 single-copy marker genes that each genome should carry once. Completeness is the share of markers found; contamination is how many appear more than once. MIMAG calls a MAG high quality at >90% completeness and <5% contamination.

Wrap-up

From who is there to what they do

DNA shows potential; RNA and metabolites show activity

Metagenomics (DNA)

  • Which organisms and genes are present
  • Includes dead cells and dormant organisms
  • Functional potential: pathways via HUMAnN, resistance genes via CARD
  • DNA is stable, so sampling is simple
  • Recovers genomes (MAGs) of uncultured species

Metatranscriptomics (RNA)

  • Which genes are being expressed right now
  • Separates active from merely present organisms
  • RNA degrades fast; samples need immediate preservation
  • rRNA must be depleted, as in Part 3
  • Often paired with metabolomics to measure outputs

Modern frontiers

  • Complete genomes from mixtures. PacBio HiFi and nanopore reads now assemble many microbes into circular, closed chromosomes directly from stool or soil.
  • Global genome catalogues. Collections such as the Unified Human Gastrointestinal Genome (UHGG) and GTDB hold hundreds of thousands of MAGs, most from species never cultured.
  • Clinical metagenomics. Untargeted sequencing of blood, spinal fluid or respiratory samples identifies pathogens, including unexpected ones, within a day.
  • Environmental DNA. eDNA in water or air is used to monitor biodiversity, invasive species and wastewater for disease surveillance.

Simulations use simplified models for teaching: 16S region coordinates follow E. coli numbering, communities are log-normal, rarefaction uses the exact hypergeometric expectation, the denoiser is an abundance-ratio rule in the spirit of UNOISE, and binning uses k-means on GC and log coverage.
