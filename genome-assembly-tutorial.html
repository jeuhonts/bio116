Stage 1 of 5

Shotgun sequencing and coverage

Random reads, and how many you need

In shotgun sequencing, reads come from random positions in the genome. Their total length divided by the genome length is the coverage, c = N × L / G. Because placement is random, some bases get covered many times and others not at all.

Lander and Waterman (1988) worked out what to expect. The fraction of the genome left uncovered is about e−c, and the number of separate covered islands (the best case for contigs) is about N × e−c. More coverage closes gaps fast, but never quite to zero.

Stage 2 of 5

Overlap–layout–consensus

The shredded-newspaper approach

If the end of one read matches the start of another, they probably came from neighbouring positions. Overlap–layout–consensus (OLC) assemblers compare every read with every other read to find these overlaps, arrange the reads into a chain (the layout), and vote at each position to get the final sequence (the consensus).

This worked well for Sanger reads and works again for long reads (Canu, and the string-graph assembler hifiasm). With a billion short reads, though, comparing all pairs becomes far too slow.

Stage 3 of 5

De Bruijn graphs

Break reads into k-mers, then follow the path

Short-read assemblers such as SPAdes, Velvet and MEGAHIT skip the all-against-all comparison. They cut every read into overlapping words of length k (k-mers). Each k-mer becomes an edge joining its first k−1 bases to its last k−1 bases. Identical k-mers from different reads collapse onto the same edge, so the graph size depends on the genome, not the number of reads.

A path that uses every edge once spells the genome. Stretches with no branching become contigs (unitigs). Choosing k is a trade-off: small k tangles the graph at repeats, while large k needs longer, error-free reads.

Stage 4 of 5

Repeats and scaffolding

The real reason assemblies fall apart

About half the human genome is repetitive: Alu elements (~300 bp, over a million copies), LINE-1 elements (up to ~6 kb), segmental duplications (often tens of kb), and centromeric satellites spanning megabases. A repeat can only be placed correctly if some read starts in unique sequence on one side and reaches unique sequence on the other. Otherwise the assembler must stop the contig at the repeat, and all copies collapse into one.

Scaffolding uses long-range links to order and orient contigs even when the sequence between them is unknown. Read pairs with a known insert size, mate-pairs, optical maps and Hi-C contacts all bridge gaps. Unknown sequence is filled with Ns.

Stage 5 of 5

Judging an assembly

Contiguity, completeness, correctness

Sort contigs from longest to shortest and add up their lengths. The contig length at which the running total first passes half the assembly is the N50. The number of contigs needed to get there is the L50. A higher N50 means a more contiguous assembly.

N50 measures only contiguity. Joining two contigs that do not belong together raises N50 while making the assembly worse. So assemblies are also checked for completeness (BUSCO: the share of expected single-copy genes found intact) and correctness (QV: Phred-scaled base accuracy, plus misassembly counts from tools such as QUAST or Merqury).

Wrap-up

Two families of assemblers

Pick the algorithm that fits the reads

Overlap / string graph

  • Nodes are reads; edges are overlaps
  • Keeps full read length, so it resolves longer repeats
  • All-pairs overlap is costly; long-read tools use minimizer sketches to speed it up
  • Examples: Canu, hifiasm, Flye (repeat graph), Verkko
  • Best for PacBio HiFi and Oxford Nanopore reads

De Bruijn graph

  • Nodes are (k−1)-mers; edges are k-mers
  • Memory grows with genome size, not read count
  • Discards read context beyond k, so repeats longer than k tangle
  • Examples: SPAdes, Velvet, MEGAHIT, ABySS
  • Best for Illumina short reads, bacteria and metagenomes

Modern frontiers

  • Telomere-to-telomere human genome. The T2T consortium's CHM13 assembly (2022) closed the last ~8% of the human reference, including centromeres, using ultra-long nanopore and HiFi reads.
  • Pangenomes. The Human Pangenome Reference Consortium (2023) replaced a single reference with a graph of many phased diploid assemblies, so variation is represented directly.
  • Haplotype-resolved assembly. Trio data or Hi-C lets tools like hifiasm and Verkko build the maternal and paternal copies separately instead of one blended consensus.
  • Biodiversity genomics. Projects such as the Earth BioGenome Project and Darwin Tree of Life aim to assemble reference genomes for all eukaryotic species.

Simulations use simplified models for teaching: coverage is uniform-random, the repeat model assumes a read must span a repeat plus 100 bp of unique flank, and scaffolding links need 300 bp of unique anchor on each side.
