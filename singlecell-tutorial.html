Stage 1 of 5

Capture and barcodes

Tagging every molecule with its cell of origin

In droplet methods such as 10x Genomics Chromium, a microfluidic chip wraps single cells in oil droplets together with a gel bead. Each bead carries millions of primers that share one cell barcode and each have a random unique molecular identifier (UMI). Inside the droplet the cell is lysed, its poly(A) mRNA is captured, and reverse transcription stamps every cDNA with the barcode and a UMI. Then all droplets are pooled and sequenced together.

Cells land in droplets at random, following a Poisson distribution. Most droplets stay empty. Loading more cells fills more droplets but also puts two cells in one droplet more often. Such a doublet looks like a single cell that expresses both cells' genes.

Stage 2 of 5

Cell quality control

Deciding which barcodes are real, healthy, single cells

A run produces far more barcodes than cells. Empty droplets still pick up a little ambient RNA from lysed cells, so they appear with a few dozen UMIs. Plotting UMIs per barcode from highest to lowest gives a knee plot: real cells form the plateau, and empties form the long tail.

Among real cells, dying cells leak cytoplasmic mRNA but keep mitochondrial transcripts, so they show a high % mitochondrial reads and few genes. Doublets tend to have unusually many UMIs. Tools like scDblFinder find doublets more reliably by comparing each cell with simulated doublets.

Stage 3 of 5

Normalization, feature selection and PCA

From 20,000 noisy genes to a handful of informative axes

Each cell's counts are divided by its total, scaled to 10,000, and log-transformed, so cells captured with different efficiency become comparable. Most genes vary only by sampling noise, which is large when a cell has just a few thousand UMIs. Highly variable genes (HVGs), those more variable than their average expression predicts, carry the biological signal.

Principal component analysis then compresses the HVGs into a few axes that capture the main patterns of co-expression. Downstream steps use the top PCs, which also averages away much of the noise.

Stage 4 of 5

Clustering and embedding

Grouping similar cells and drawing a map

Standard pipelines (Seurat, Scanpy) build a nearest-neighbour graph in PC space and split it into communities with the Leiden algorithm. A resolution parameter sets how finely it splits. Here we use k-means with a chosen number of clusters, which behaves similarly for teaching.

To look at the data, cells are embedded in 2D with t-SNE or UMAP. Both keep neighbours close together, but distances between far-apart clusters and cluster sizes on the plot don't mean much. Always cluster in PC space, not on the 2D picture.

Stage 5 of 5

Annotating cell types

Putting names on clusters

Clusters are just numbers until you name them. The usual approach is to find each cluster's marker genes and match them to known biology. A dot plot shows two things for each gene and cluster: dot size is the share of cells with any UMI for that gene, and colour is the average expression. Reference-based tools (Azimuth, CellTypist, SingleR) automate this by transferring labels from an annotated atlas.

Wrap-up

Droplets and plates

Throughput versus depth per cell

Droplet-based (10x Chromium, similar)

  • Thousands to hundreds of thousands of cells per run
  • 3′ or 5′ tag counting with UMIs
  • ~1,000–5,000 genes detected per cell
  • Low cost per cell; best for cell-type discovery and atlases
  • Isoforms and SNVs mostly out of reach

Plate-based full-length (Smart-seq3)

  • Hundreds of cells, sorted one per well
  • Coverage across the whole transcript
  • More genes per cell; resolves isoforms and allele-specific expression
  • Higher cost and labour per cell
  • Best for rare, deeply profiled populations

Modern frontiers

  • Multi-omics per cell. CITE-seq adds surface protein counts; 10x Multiome measures RNA and chromatin accessibility (ATAC) in the same nucleus.
  • Integration and atlases. Harmony, scVI and similar tools correct batch effects across donors and labs, powering the Human Cell Atlas and Tabula Sapiens.
  • Perturb-seq. CRISPR guides are read out alongside each cell's transcriptome, testing thousands of gene knockouts in one experiment.
  • Spatial and foundation models. Spatial platforms map cell types in intact tissue, and models trained on tens of millions of cells (for example scGPT and Geneformer) are being tested for annotation and perturbation prediction.

The dataset is simulated: six PBMC types with Poisson counts from fixed expression profiles, plus doublets, dying cells and ambient-RNA empty droplets. Clustering uses k-means as a stand-in for Leiden, and the embedding is an exact t-SNE computed in your browser.
